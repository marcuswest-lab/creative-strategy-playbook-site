What the job is

Make winning ads (and funnels). In practice that means:

  • Write ads that resonate with the audience, and make them different enough that the algorithm serves them to different people.
  • Make them fun, creative, persuasive, and aimed at the right people.
  • Know the market deeply enough to talk the way they talk.
  • Keep up with winning formats and styles, and apply them to the brand.
  • Watch for patterns in winners and for gaps nobody has filled.
  • Place strategic bets: variations vs net-new, proven vs wildcard, which sources.
  • Write strong hooks and know direct-response fundamentals well enough to execute.

Our account rules (Marcus)

Luke teaches the method. These rules come from our own accounts and sit on top of it:

  1. Every ad is one cheap swap from something proven. In order of evidence: our winner → a competitor's long-running ad → a comment → an organic outlier hook → pure invention.
  2. Evidence order: our account's results → category data (Parker, Ad Library run-days) → expert opinion.
  3. A winner must clear the bottom line (CAC or ROAS), not just a leading metric.
  4. Fund tests properly. Fewer tests that reach the account's spend threshold beat many starved ones.
  5. Turn one knob, and name it, so the result is readable.
  6. Account-specific overrides live with the account, for example ED-CREATIVE-NOTES.md in the ED project.

Taste and instinct

Your long-term goal is a sixth sense for what will work: it operates below language because you notice patterns too subtle to explain (Luke cites chicken sexers and Blink). With AI producing unlimited options, this judgment is the job, which Luke calls being "paid to feel."

How to train it:

  • Saturate your brain in the account's winners and in audience language, repeatedly. Copy top ads into a running doc and read them.
  • Feel it in your body (interoception). Read two headlines and notice which one lifts something in your chest or gut. Notice how you get sold to.
  • Gray vs color. In any ad, mark what's genuinely interesting versus boilerplate. The colored parts are the seeds for new ads.
  • Study everything: tabloids, black-hat direct response, big brands, brain rot. Anywhere humans pay attention.

The research process

Do it at onboarding, then again every few weeks. Read the sources yourself first, then have AI process everything; each catches what the other misses.

Step 1: set the research question

Start from what the account needs: a stronger message for current buyers, a new presentation, or a new audience. "Why do people hesitate after seeing the price?" beats "find hooks."

Step 2: collect the market's own words

Where What to look for
Comments on our ads and competitors' ads (Luke's #1) Questions, arguments, skepticism, home remedies, high-like comments
Reviews: direct competitors, products with the same outcome, products built on one of our key ingredients (Luke's #2) Five-star outcomes, one-star letdowns, why they chose that brand
Reddit, forums and Facebook groups Long first-person stories, failed attempts, the replies
Sales calls, surveys, support tickets Why now, what they tried, objections in their words
Organic outliers (a burner feed, below) What the segment already pays attention to, plus its comments
Competitor ads and pages Promises, mechanisms, proof and offers already in the market (sophistication)
News and new papers Dated events and findings that can anchor a hook (verify before using)
Our own account Which concepts, angles and CASTS combinations are actually converting

Save the exact words, the source link and the context.

Step 3: sort what you find

Pull out Becomes
Objects they mention Images, props, scenes
Narrative arcs Story ads
Body states Pain in their words
Symbols Visual shorthand
Settings (hospital, bedroom, stairwell) Terrain and scenarios

D2C Diaries' buckets for the brief: angles · benefits · pain points · desired outcomes · objections · purchase triggers · misconceptions · stories. Go beneath the surface pain: "tired" means struggling at work for one person and no energy for their kids for another.

Step 4: answer the buyer questions (Stefan Georgi's RMBC)

Area Questions
Customer Who are they? How old? Hopes and dreams? Victories and failures?
Beliefs What outside forces do they believe kept them from their best life? Their core beliefs in one to three sentences?
Existing solutions What are they already using? What has that been like? What do they like and dislike about it?
Skepticism Do they believe existing solutions work? If not, why?
Curiosity Has someone tried to solve this in a very unusual way? What happened?
Perceived change Do they believe the problem used to not exist, or not be so bad? That outside forces made it worse?

Then RMBC continues: Mechanism (what the alternatives are missing and what ours does differently) → Brief (buyer → problem → promise → explanation → proof → offer) → Copy.

Step 5: find the trigger moments (Mike Rinard)

Character → emotions → trigger moments. Who are they, and who matters to them? What do they desire, what pains them, what do they fear? What real-life moment brings it to the surface (a client saying "we need to talk," an unexpected bill, struggling on the stairs)? A trigger moment is a scene the viewer recognizes, not a label like "financial stress." These become story openings.

Step 6: map the worldview

What does the segment already believe, who are its enemies, and which debates is it in? (See 1c · Persona bank.) Unaware ads are built on this.

Output: the persona bank (1c · Persona bank) plus a research doc of quotes sorted into the buckets above.

Organic: a burner feed for viral hooks

Make a separate Instagram or TikTok account and train its algorithm to match the prospect: follow the right accounts, watch their most popular videos to the end, open the comments. Then look for outliers: videos doing many times their creator's normal views. Swipe the hook, connect it to your argument, and read those comments too.

If every idea comes from competitors, your own head or templates, your hit rate drops. Pull from all of these:

T Templates: proven ad structures that have worked for years

1b · Patterns and gaps Back to top ↑

1b. Patterns and gaps

Sources: creative strategy in 15 minutes, the "boring" ad moves, what "strategy" means.

The whole job, at its simplest: look at what's working in your account, competitors' accounts and the wider market, and do more of it, while filling in what's missing.

Patterns: what keeps showing up in winners

A pattern is any significant element you keep seeing in winning (or losing) ads: an actor, a format, a style, a concept, a mechanism. Ask of each ad: why is this working? Why is this kind of working? Why is this not working? Then form and test a hypothesis.

How to find them:

  1. Put your top spenders and new ads with signs of life side by side (20 or so).
  2. Break each into its anatomy (2a · Ad anatomy).
  3. Ask what they share: "three winners mention this concept," "two use this angle."
  4. Hypothesize why, then build the next ads on it.

We aren't in a lab, so hold patterns loosely and look for the variables strong enough to survive the noise.

Rotate elements around the winning one

Decide which element you think is carrying the ad, keep it, and change the others:

You think it's working because of… Keep it, and try…
The actor (e.g. the escort) The same actor in new concepts, angles and conceits
The concept ("men cheat when the wife stops glowing") New angles, formats, actors and styles on that concept
The combination (this concept × this actor) Hold both; change the angle, mechanism, hook, or the conceit (podcast, on stage, street interview, slice-of-life film)
The format (timeline) More timeline ads on other concepts

The basic formula: new creative on winning messaging, or new messaging on winning creative. New messaging and new creative together can produce blockbusters, but the hit rate is low.

Gaps: what's missing

You only see a gap if you can break ads into their anatomy. Compare your account with competitors, or with the theoretical ideal:

Gap type Example
Format Competitors run lots of timeline ads; we run none
Style Competitors have a new style we haven't tried
Segment / facet A segment buried in our comments or Amazon reviews that nobody targets
Awareness No unaware long-form statics
Concept A concept family competitors haven't touched

The matrix: list every possible segment × awareness level × format × style, and mark what's been tested. Blank cells are candidate gaps.

Internal vectors

Luke's favourite source: dump your winning (and losing) ads into AI and ask what the patterns are, then add your own read. AI alone tends to find the same ideas again; your feedback is what gives it a new seed.

1c. The persona bank: who, awareness and sophistication

Sources: creative strategy in 15 minutes, 16 god-tier formats (facets), unaware ads masterclass, 7 hidden sources for statics (the 5×5 matrix).

A segment is a slice of the market: outcome × demographic × facet

Dimension What it is Examples (testosterone product)
Outcome The core thing they want, or want gone Better sex, lose man boobs, more energy, stronger erections
Demographic Gender, age, race, who the buyer is Men 50+, Black men, the wife buying for him
Facet A habit, trait, identity or condition Cigarette smoker, on finasteride, considering TRT, just off TRT, on GLP-1, vegan, keto, runner, diabetic

Multiply as few or as many as you like: "all men who want better sex" is broad; "men over 50 on finasteride who want to lose weight" is narrow. In accounts with big spend, narrow facets are where a lot of the extra gains are.

Horizontal scaling means finding new outcomes and sub-outcomes, then multiplying facets, and writing ads specifically for those people. Most of the biggest gains come from here.

Awareness: where the ad starts the conversation

Level They know… The ad's job
Unaware Nothing about the problem, or they aren't thinking about it Capture attention with high curiosity, then make them care
Problem aware They have a problem, not that solutions exist Articulate the problem sharply, then introduce the solution type
Solution aware Solutions exist, not yours Position your product type as the best option
Product aware Your product, but not convinced Build value and differentiate
Most aware Your product; they need the offer Offer, price, urgency

For writing, Luke lumps these into two buckets: unaware + problem aware (curiosity-led, native-looking, more variety, scales further) and solution + product aware (product-centred, proven templates). Unaware ads are the hardest to write and the biggest pool of buyers. See the unaware hook types.

The diversity matrix: segments × awareness levels. Five segments × five levels = 25 buckets. True creative diversity means covering many buckets, not clustering in the obvious ones.

Market sophistication: what they've already heard

Awareness is what they know. Sophistication is how many claims they've already seen (Eugene Schwartz's stages, as summarized by Nick Theriot):

Stage The market What works
1 First to market A simple claim
2 Second or third A bigger claim
3 Crowded; obvious solutions tried A new mechanism
4 Mechanisms copied An enlarged or proven mechanism
5 Burned out; every claim sounds like a lie Identify with their frustration; insight and story

Worldview: the layer under the segment

Unaware ads in particular work by speaking to the segment's worldview: what they already believe, who their enemies are, which debates they're in. Luke's frame (after Blair Warren) is that people respond to those who encourage their dreams, justify their failures, allay their fears, confirm their suspicions and help them throw rocks at their enemies. Also: give them a mission, an identity to be proud of, permission to want, and clarity when things feel uncertain.

The persona bank (one per account / offer)

Build macro personas and the micro personas inside them. For each, record:

  1. Segment (outcome × demographic × facet)
  2. Awareness level
  3. Market sophistication
  4. TAM: an estimate with source, date and assumptions, or "unknown"
  5. Biggest fears, pains, concerns, desires and motivations for buying
  6. Biggest objections
  7. Worldview: beliefs, enemies, debates

Overlapping personas: never add their TAMs together. Example: the ED persona bank in the ED project (research/ed-persona-bank-v2-2026-10-22/).

Our skills: horizontal-scaling (persona and facet distinctions for new segments).

Why the grammar matters

  • "Format" hides the structure. People call things "timeline ads," "podcast ads," "monk ads," "Pixar song ads." Each name points at a different element:
What people call it What it actually names
Timeline ad Structure (format)
Podcast ad Conceit
Monk ad / Dr. Pickle ad Actor (× conceit)
Pixar song ad Style × conceit
Skeleton timeline ad Actor × structure
  • Elements stack. A timeline, inside a podcast, delivered by a skeleton monk, in Pixar style, sung. You don't have to stack them all, but once you see the grammar, combining them is easy.
  • It makes patterns visible, to you and to AI. Tag ads by these elements and you can see whether a format, style, concept or mechanism is the thing that's working.
  • It makes variations easy. Keep the element you believe is winning and rotate the rest (see 1b · Patterns and gaps).

2b. Big idea, concept and angle

Sources: creative strategy in 15 minutes, insight vectors, why pain points are dead, copy instincts pt. 1, and Stefan Georgi's big idea vs mechanism vs concept vs angle vs hook (CA Pro call notes).

This is the first planning step: decide what idea the ad sells before choosing its format and CASTS. The research and persona bank (workflow 1) feed it; the hooks and copy (workflow 3) express it.

The hierarchy: big idea → mechanism → concept → angle → hook

Stefan's dead-simple separation of terms that usually get blurred:

Level What it is How many Example (skincare)
Big idea One idea that's instantly understood as exciting, important and beneficial, so buying becomes the obvious next step. It has "dinner-table" shareability: explainable in 10 seconds One per offer or campaign. It anchors every ad and the funnel "The biggest cause of wrinkles isn't age or genetics, it's washing your face in the bathroom sink"
Mechanism The scientific or technical how that proves the big idea is true One per big idea Chloramine in tap water strips the skin's protective lipid barrier
Concept (Stefan's sense) The vehicle that dramatizes the big idea 5–10 per big idea A raw iPhone confession from a 50-year-old woman · a lab test of sink water under a microscope · a whistleblower aesthetician
Angle The entry point inside a concept: the villain, the symptom, the lens Several per concept The chlorine in city water · moisturizers that sting after washing · women in hard-water states age faster
Hook The first 3–5 seconds: stop the scroll and buy the next 10 seconds Many per angle "If you live in Florida and wash your face in the sink, you need to hear this before tonight…"

Pimsleur's big idea is another example: "To learn a language as an adult, mimic how a child learns." Its mechanism is graduated interval recall.

Watch the terms. Luke and Stefan use "concept" differently. Luke's concept is the core idea of one ad ("too much vitamin D is bad for you"): a slice of the big idea. Stefan's concept is the packaging, which in this playbook is the format (2c) plus CASTS (2e). Both use "angle" the same way. The rest of this playbook uses Luke's meaning.

How to find the big idea: start from the research (1a) and the persona bank (1c), then use copy instincts and insight vectors below. The test: can you say it in one sentence, does it instantly make sense, and if the prospect accepts it, is our product the only logical next step?

Concept (Luke): the one core idea of each ad

The concept is the single idea the ad communicates, e.g. "too much vitamin D is bad for you." Write it in plain caveman words. If you can't, the ad has no point yet. Every ad has (or should have) one concept, and the hook usually expresses it. Knowing the concept is how you see which ideas the market responds to.

Pain points aren't a concept. In a mature market everyone already knows the pains and the benefits. There's no edge in writing them slightly better. An ad needs an insight: a reason to exist, a new idea about the problem or the solution.

Angle: how you present the idea

The same concept through different lenses. "Too much vitamin D is bad for you" could be:

  • a conspiracy about the vitamin D industry
  • a whistleblower
  • two people, one with and one without, and what happened to each

Other common angles: contrarian, story, paradoxical question, new discovery, lost wisdom, expert-based, comparison, test or experiment, personified object, confession.

Mechanism: why it works

Part The question
UMP (unique mechanism of the problem) Why do I have this problem?
UMS (unique mechanism of the solution) Why is this the right fix for that?

The best mechanisms are simple, intuitive and new. Usually one problem and one solution. The more you tie in a coherent story (the founder, how the product was made, unique ingredients or features, characterizations, a moment in time), the stronger the positioning.

Copy instincts: build the world where your product is the obvious answer. Ask: "What would have to be true about the world for this to be the best possible solution?" Then work backwards:

  1. What's most unique about this product (ingredient, combination, delivery, spokesperson, packaging)?
  2. How can those elements be preeminent: the most, the best, a category of one?
  3. Which associations and "magical thinking" make them feel even more special? (Luke's example: salmon PDRN reframed as Korean cherry salmon, whose skin survives the swim upstream.)
  4. Why is this combination right for this time and place (culture, zeitgeist)?
  5. Does it all lock together coherently?

Psychology over facts: start from what would be most psychologically compelling to the prospect, then find the facts that make it true. Don't lie or mislead. Just don't limit the search to the ingredient list.

Insight vectors: how to generate insights on demand

People carry mental models of how the world works ("X causes Y," "more A means more B"). An insight finds a weak spot in that model and adds a new piece. Common vectors:

Vector Example shape
Reverse causation "Your weight slows your metabolism, not the other way round"
Multiple causes "It's not one thing, it's A, B and C together"
Hidden constraint "The real limit isn't willpower, it's X"
Hidden condition "It works, but only if Y stays true"
Subtract, don't add "You don't need more A, you need less C"
Context flip "X is good, except here, where it's bad"
Archetype / type "What's your metabolism type?"
Leading indicator "This small number predicts the big outcome weeks early"
Virtuous / vicious cycle "Each time you use it, it compounds" / "it spirals"

Intensification is the beat that follows: show how the problem compounds over time if nothing changes, and how the benefit compounds once it's fixed.

When to use a story

Starting awareness Where the story starts What it must establish
Unaware A recognizable moment, desire or event, before they've named the problem Why this moment matters → what explains it → why the offer is relevant

Step 1: build the dramatic premise first

Beat Question
Core wound What does the practical problem mean emotionally? Feeling unwanted, unreliable, left out, not the parent or partner they want to be
Dramatic scenario Which concrete event reveals it through action?
Raise the stakes What could be lost, and who's watching? Have it happen in public or at an important event (a wedding, reunion, anniversary, party)
Dramatic question "Will this person overcome X before Y?"

Story variations that work (change the discovery, order or ending): someone you know · found it · inside job · rekindled · run-in (show the payoff first, then explain) · countdown · second chance · pilgrimage · authority · stranger intervention. Luke's current winning subtypes include revenge stories (cheating spouse), an upcoming event ("I got invited to this party"), interrogation scenes (stuck at the DMV), and the future self coming back.

Step 2: map the beats (narrative ads)

Movement What it must do Beats to choose from
Recognition A person, an event, an unresolved problem that matters Cold open · backstory (only what's needed) · wound · escalating stakes · previous attempts and dismissal · rock bottom
Discovery Earn a change in understanding and connect it to the offer Turn · messenger · hidden cause · why nobody explained it · skepticism and proof
Resolution Show the change, then make the offer clear Product entry · first use · timeline · witness · payoff · reader pivot · offer · P.S.

Not every ad needs all 18 beats. Keep the ones that make the argument clear and true.

Borrowing from a reference story: read the whole thing, list its actual beats in order (what happens, each beat's job, its position, the narrator, and every detail that returns later), then write ours beside it with new events and language, beat for beat.

Step 3: write micro dramas (dramatic shorts)

Beat Job Question
Disruption Open inside a consequential event What just happened that the viewer needs explained?
Stakes Make the personal or relationship cost clear with minimal history What does each person think this means?
Complication A choice or piece of evidence makes it harder What changes because of the last beat?
Reversal Reveal something that changes what we saw Which assumption fails, and where was it seeded?
Decision and offer bridge A motivated action connects to a real product fact Why does the offer belong here rather than appear from nowhere?
Payoff and next step Answer the emotional question, return to a planted detail, give the CTA What changed, and what can the viewer actually do?

Writing rules: open on action, evidence or confrontation · give characters agency, so their choices cause the next complication · vary the tension (a discovery, a changed interpretation, a hard decision) · let props, gestures and silence carry information · keep exchanges short; subtext beats explaining every emotion · pay off questions within the ad (no serial cliffhanger).

Three routes: acted (competing goals drive the dialogue) · narrated (the narrator adds interpretation while visuals show events; don't describe everything twice) · sung (a Suno song; each verse advances the story).

Techniques worth carrying into every story

  • Event-led opening: start at the moment something happens; the second sentence develops or flips the tension.
  • Small event, real stakes: specific actions and avoided moments, not tragedy for its own sake.
  • Backstory earns its space: if removing it doesn't hurt the turn, cut it.
  • A credible messenger: the explanation matches what the speaker could actually know.
  • Picture the explanation: a short analogy for the mechanism (it doesn't replace proof).
  • Plant and pay off: an object, place or phrase from the opening returns at the end.
  • Fiction stays fiction: a dramatized scene is never presented as a real customer testimonial.

Reusable story brief

Offer and next step · audience and the one selling idea · what's fixed vs being tested · runtime and route · protagonist's want and hidden concern · the other character's want or misreading · opening event and visible clue · complication · seeded reversal and decision · product bridge and the proof it needs · payoff, returning detail, CTA · beat-by-beat shot plan (line, action, reaction, duration).

Before production: does the opening make sense with no backstory? Does every beat change something? Was the reveal set up? Does the ending answer the main question? Is it intelligible without sound? Is every product fact accurate for this offer?

Winning Ad Styles — the list

A style is the filter the ad is rendered through. Same script, same claims, same offer, same structure, different picture: Pixar, camcorder, Notes app, courtroom sketch. Nothing the buyer has to believe changes when you swap it, which is why it's the cheapest swap there is. You are not writing a new ad. You are re-rendering a proven one.

Three rules. 1. Match the skin to the awareness: cold traffic wants native, ugly, low-fidelity (camcorder, Notes app, raw UGC); product-aware traffic can carry polish. 2. Some styles do the targeting for you: a reggae track, a country kitchen, an anime skin pick the audience before a word lands.

How to use this tonight

  1. Take a winner, or anything with real signal. Freeze the script.
  2. Pick five styles from the list your account has never run.
  3. Brief each one: same words, same order, same offer; only the rendering changes. Note where a style break belongs, if anywhere.
  4. Render and launch. Every one is one swap from proven, so you know what the test measures.
  5. A style wins → it's the next winner in; pick five more.

3a. Hooks

Sources: the 4-step "deep fry" hook process, 8 vicious hook rules, 6 curiosity triggers, unaware ads masterclass, and Marcus's Luke Iha hook workshop transcript.

The hook is roughly 90% of the messaging. Spend most of your writing time on it, and learn to write hooks by hand: a few hundred reps and you can write them in seconds. Once the hook is right, AI can write most of the rest.

The four steps

Step What to do
1. Isolate the idea Write the ad (or a basic hook), then ask "what am I actually trying to say?" Put it in caveman words: "hot celebrities don't drink coffee," "menopause strips collagen." Rate it. If it's obvious (or the market already believes they know it), it needs work.
2. Commit to it One idea per hook. Don't hedge by adding more ideas. "Can you guess what these hot celebrities have in common? None of them drink coffee" is already two ideas.
3. Deep fry it Make it the most bizarre, extended, extreme version that still has a thread back to the truth, then walk it back.
4. Make it bang Strongest material first, specific, active voice, one to three emotional trigger words, an open loop.

Mindset for step 3: use associative, squishy logic (not strict logic); don't be a nerd (psychology over facts); use the tightrope effect (say something so bold they have to watch you pay it off); go crazy first, then walk it back. Most ads die from boring ideas, not wild ones. Never lie or mislead: the body has to pay the hook off honestly.

Deep-fry moves:

Move Example
Good thing → bad Zinc "helps immunity" → too much zinc starves you of copper → the "zinc flu"
Bad thing → good "I'm the worst dad of all time, and I'm proud of it"
Ineffective → harmful "It doesn't just not work, it makes it worse" (only if true)
Identity contrast "My grandma's weird naked magnesium trick"
Reverse causation "If you're diabetic, your cells are starving for sugar"
Types of types (nuance) Brown fat vs grey fat; "dermal menopause"
Rename (characterization) "Naked magnesium," "joint drought," "ice water trick"

Underneath them: contrast, surprise, nuance and stakes create attention.

Luke's magnesium example: magnesium cream is better than capsules → it absorbs through skin because it has no molecule attached → it's "bare" → "naked magnesium" → "My grandma used this weird naked magnesium trick that fixed her back pain in two weeks." The grandma/naked contrast, "weird" and "naked" as charged words, and "trick" as an open loop make it work.

The 8 vicious hook rules

A vicious hook grabs a feeling the reader already has (a fear, a shame, an unspoken dread) and squeezes it in the very first line. Most people start their ad three or four sentences in. A polite or comfortable hook is a dead hook.

  1. Relevant in the first line (first 10–20 words). Three ways in: pain or condition callout (including a mechanism they already believe in, like cortisol); belief callout (take a side in a debate they're already having: "they told you red meat would clog your arteries"); general openness (soap-opera intrigue, the hardest and biggest bets).
  2. Emotionally charged language: banned, sexless, stink, lying, die, naked, killing, terrifying, beg.
  3. Real open loops: if they can guess the answer, they won't read on.
  4. Stakes and rubbernecking: something heavy is on the line.
  5. Specific and tangible, like seasoning: enough to feel real, not so much it crowds the line.
  6. Doesn't read like an ad: news, a story, a confession, an insight.
  7. Caveman, Germanic words: sweat, not perspire; bad, not severe; sleepless, not insomnia.
  8. Consequence first, mechanism later. "This morning habit causes electrical imbalance" means nothing to them; "this morning habit spikes cortisol" does.

Six forms of tangible curiosity

Cycle through these before writing any new ad:

Form Example
Metaphor (natural, mechanical, object) "Your dog's brain is like a radio picking up a million signals"
Paradoxical question "Why does your dog pull harder when you pull back?"
Characterization (a concrete, visual nickname) Ice water trick, purple peel, thousand buyers a day
Testable proof "Press your thumb into your shin for 5 seconds…"
Quiz "Which of these jobs has the highest risk of tooth decay?"
Surprising culprit (the problem behind the problem) "The one object in your bathroom sabotaging your weight loss," "why your cutting board could be making you fat"

Start from the curiosity and then connect it back to the product ("brick logic"). There's almost always a true connection.

Unaware hooks: high curiosity, low-to-medium relevance

Unaware ads lead with something highly curious that's only loosely tied to their problem: "My Uber driver said five words that changed my view on men," "In 1910, Rockefeller funded a report that reshaped American medicine." The more specific the relevance ("to your body" → "to your blood sugar" → "to your neuropathy"), the narrower the audience; the more open it is, the stronger the curiosity has to be.

Ten types that keep working: paradoxes · lost wisdom / forgotten remedy · conspiracy · warning sign · everyday threat (surprising culprit) · story · big win (the benefit behind the benefit) · big horror · symptom callout · sacred cow (validate or challenge a belief).

Built-in curiosity triggers: NASA, Harvard, the Nobel Prize, the Amish, Japan, the Swiss, monks, tribes, ancient Egyptians, Navy SEALs, the CIA, blue zones.

Rules that override the method

  • If you're swiping, just swipe. The market has spoken: use the exact hook.
  • Same for organic outliers: borrow the hook as-is (see 1a · Research the market).
  • Not every hook needs to be crazy. Sometimes picking the right format makes the hook fall out on its own ("here's what happens when…").
  • Our account rule: give a proven body 8–15 new hooks before writing a new body, and test viral hooks only on a proven body.

3b. Copy blocks and proof

Sources: creative strategy in 15 minutes, the 22 proof weapons, copy instincts pt. 1.

Once the idea and angle are chosen (2b) and the hook is written (3a), the body copy is built from these blocks.

Copy blocks: the building blocks of persuasion

Block What it does
Pain What they want to get rid of, in lived detail
Promise What they want to gain, anchored to one core transformation
Proof The reason to believe; pair it with every promise
Curiosity What's interesting and new: usually the mechanism, the bridge from pain to promise
Constraints What holds them back (time, money, effort, beliefs), removed
Conditions Urgency, scarcity, discounts: why now

Use them like a checklist: more blocks, and stronger ones, usually make a stronger ad. Most copy feedback comes down to adding a missing block or strengthening a weak one.

Proof: 22 types in 5 categories

Four rules: (1) match the proof to the size of the promise; (2) the harder proof is to fake, the stronger it is; (3) let them experience it themselves when possible; (4) psychological proof, making it feel true, is the most underrated kind.

Category Types
Psychological Technical jargon (a quick flurry, used sparingly) · candid communication (admit a flaw) · explanatory reasoning · logical argument (math) · guarantees
Experiential Testable proof ("check this right now") · demonstration · trials and samples · challenges · comparisons · before and after
Empirical Studies (and say why the source matters) · stats · infographics
Credible Third-party certifications · track record · expert endorsements · press · awards
Social Testimonials and reviews · case studies · social media metrics

Place proof where doubt creeps in: right after a big claim, right before a call to action. Proof isn't about convincing; it's about making them feel safe buying.

Regulated categories (health, ED, finance) restrict claims, testimonials and certifications. Check the account's compliance notes before using any proof type.

4. Ad production: making the ads

Sources: the 5-step workflow for 100 ads a week, creative strategy in 15 minutes, 7 hidden sources for statics.

The standard

Luke's bar is 50–100 good ads a week: about 10 ads in a 2–3 hour sprint, with a timer. Constraints stop over-editing. Only around 10–20% of ads will win, so failure is the rule: you get better through reps, not by perfecting one ad.

The workflow: 20% analysis · 70% ideation · 10% writing

Phase Share What happens
Analysis ~20% Open the account. Read the top spenders and new ads showing signs of life. Copy them into a running "winners" doc. You're collecting material and training your instincts
Ideation ~70% Set the awareness split, run STORMING, end with 20–30 ideas as one-paragraph briefs (add a hook if you have one; paste organic hooks verbatim)
Writing ~10% Hook first, then body, headlines and creative (below)

Most people get this backwards and spend half their time writing. In the age of AI, writing is the cheap part; the idea and its hook are the expensive part.

Writing: hook first

You need three lists: winning hooks (the first one or two lines), winning ads, and winning headlines.

  1. Hook: give the AI your winning hooks plus the raw idea → ask for 10–20 hooks like these → "more like #3 and #7" → edit by hand. This is 70–80% of the writing effort.
  2. Body: the chosen hook + the raw idea + winning ads → "write an ad that starts with this hook, modelled on these." Light edits only. Spend your editing on the hook and the opening.
  3. Headlines: winning headlines + hook + body → options → pick.
  4. Creative: statics through the static process (2e · CASTS); videos go to an editor with a winning reference.

Deliverable rules

Every ad meets the account's standing deliverable rules (for ED: burned-in subtitles, safe-zone text, one product per ad, verified ingredients). Labels, batch sizing and launch rules are in 5b · Ad testing.

How winning concepts grow into account pillars

New idea (often a long-form static) → it gets traction → optimize the copy and creative (statics into videos, new spokespeople, hooks, formats) → build congruent funnels (advertorial, quiz) around the copy and creative pair → a scaling pillar. A few pillars carry the account, and every pillar starts as a fresh idea from a diverse source.

How production works (high level)

There are three routes from an approved brief to a finished file. All of them end the same way: one video per ad with burned-in captions, text inside the Meta Reels safe zone, the same file used on TikTok and Meta.

Route When The steps

Editor Live action, UGC, heavy edits Write the brief (creative-brief-generator or bad-creative-brief) → Brief Intake → the editor gets the brief plus a winning reference → review against the brief and the deliverable rules

The in-house AI video pipeline:

  1. Script and shot plan: hook, body and the shot list with timing (the brief).
  2. Stills: generate the characters and key frames (Higgsfield Soul, Gemini/Nano Banana) and keep the characters consistent across shots.
  3. Motion: animate the stills into clips (Veo 3 via Google Flow, Kling, Seedance). Kling's edit mode can restyle an existing winner (e.g. the 4437 body as claymation) without re-shooting.
  4. Voice and music: voiceover (ElevenLabs, Gemini narration) or a song (Suno, Lyria); lock the audio before timing the shots.
  5. Edit and assemble: stitch the clips to the audio, retime each shot, add the opening card, product shots and B-roll. Done with an assemble.py script per ad (ffmpeg) or in CapCut.
  6. Captions: transcribe with the approved script, then render burned-in captions in the brand style (ai-media captions: preview once, reuse the style).
  7. QA: check frames for text in the safe zone, caption timing against the audio, product and claim accuracy, and no AI artifacts (Stefan's editing checks in 6). Then upload.

5a. Strategic bets: choosing what to test

Sources: what "strategy" means, the "boring" ad moves behind $100mm+, creative strategy in 15 minutes.

Ideas aren't the problem; choosing what to test first is the most important part of the job. Strategy is choice: deciding what not to do, with limited production and limited spend.

The decisions you're making (make them consciously)

  • Iterate on a winner, or make a net-new ad?
  • Which sources do I pull from?
  • More proven, or more wildcard?
  • New segments, or deeper into one segment?
  • New styles, formats or conceits?
  • What mix of awareness levels?

Use gaps + patterns + taste to answer them, and write the hypothesis down so you can look back at it later.

The most reliable moves (highest hit rate first)

Reliability Move
Highest Swap a new style onto a winning message (Pixar → claymation, same podcast)
Swap a new format or conceit onto a winning message (yapper → podcast, street interview, tier list)
Put a past winning message onto a new trending style (revive fatigued winners)
Swipe a competitor fully
Mid-range Winning message or creative → new segment (new outcome, demographic or facet)
Swipe an organic viral hook or creative onto winning messaging or creative
Turn a high-signal comment or review into an ad on winning messaging or creative
Lower hit rate, biggest upside New messaging × new creative (blockbusters live here)

The simpler the move, the better. Most people abandon ideas long before the market does.

Production plan: an example week of 10 ads

  1. Decide the split: e.g. 3 variations on the current winner (new conceits on its concept), 2 swipes, 2 segment transplants, 3 wildcards. Also choose the awareness mix: e.g. 6 unaware or problem aware, 4 solution or product aware.
  2. Run STORMING (1a · Research the market) until you have 20–30 ideas for 10 slots. Leftovers become next week's bank.
  3. Pick the 10 that best fit the plan, and record the hypothesis for each.

Spear vs net: sometimes you hunt a specific idea from your analysis (spear); sometimes you scan swipes and organic content for whatever is strongest (net). Do both, so you don't miss a banger by staring too hard at your own plan.

Mix by account stage (our rule)

Account Mix
New, or few winners Mostly net-new concepts, borrowed from the market's long-runners. For a new offer in a known category, capture existing demand first with solution- and product-aware ads
Some winners with clear gaps Roughly half variations, half new
Mature, with fresh winners About 70–75% variations, 25–30% new, never zero

5b. Ad testing: launching a batch you can learn from

Sources: Marcus's account rules (V2 Winner System and account notes), Luke's creative strategy in 15 minutes and what "strategy" means (diversity, hypotheses), 7 hidden sources for statics (creative diversity under Andromeda), Stefan Georgi's post-mortem (portfolio mix).

Testing turns the batch from 4 · Ad production into evidence. The goal isn't only to find a winner; it's to know why something won, so the next batch is smarter.

Before launch: set the rules for this account

Write these down once per account (the ED project keeps them in ED-CREATIVE-NOTES.md):

Rule What to define
Bottom-line metric What makes a real winner: CAC, ROAS, cost per sale
Leading metrics, in order The cheap early signals you judge first (e.g. cost per click or quiz start → cost per lead → cost per sale)
Spend floor How much an ad must spend before you call it
Pause rules When a test stops (e.g. no lead by a set spend; CPA above a ceiling after the first sales)
Winner threshold The number that makes an ad a winner (and a "leading winner" that only wins on early metrics)

Label every ad at launch

Label Example
Track Variation · repair · net-new
One swap from The winner, swipe, comment or organic hook it borrows from
Knob turned The one element that changed (hook, conceit, actor, style, segment…)
Persona and awareness e.g. "M1 aging husband · solution aware"
Hypothesis Why we think it will work, in one sentence
Decides it The bottom-line metric and threshold

Tag the anatomy too (segment, concept, angle, format, CASTS), ideally in the ad name, so patterns are visible later (1b · Patterns and gaps).

Size and structure the batch

  • Fund tests properly. Fewer ads that each reach the spend floor beat many starved ones. If the budget can't carry every ad to the floor, make the batch smaller.
  • Turn one knob per variation, and name it, so the result is readable. A combined change is logged as combined.
  • Compare like with like. Test a variation next to its parent, in the same or an identical ad group. Group context alone can shift results 1.5–2× (seen in the ED account).
  • Make net-new ads genuinely different. Meta's Andromeda system rewards creative diversity. Same actor, same room, new words can read as the same ad. When the goal is new reach, change visible signals together (actor, setting, first frame) and cover different segment × awareness buckets.
  • Keep a balanced portfolio. Mostly proven-based work with a deliberate share of swings: by account stage (see 5a · Strategic bets), or Stefan's 80/20.
  • Expect most to fail. Roughly 10–20% of ads win. A failed test with a clear label is still useful.

While it runs

  • Judge leading metrics first, then sales; don't kill an ad before its spend floor unless a pause rule says so.
  • Don't edit a live test mid-flight. Launch a new variant instead, so the comparison stays clean.
  • Watch for delivery problems (rejections, no spend) separately from performance. An ad that never delivered hasn't been tested.

Hand-off to evaluation

When a test reaches its floor or a pause rule, record the result against its label (metric, spend, verdict) and move to 6 · Evaluate and iterate.

6. Evaluate, iterate, refine, and have fun

Several times a week, look at your account, competitor accounts and the market. Let the data and your judgment refine your focus. Most ads fail; keep going.

What happened Path

Knowing whether an ad won is easy. The real work is knowing why it won or lost, so you can keep, iterate or drop the hypothesis behind it.

The metrics to look at

Read them top to bottom: attention, then clicks, then conversion, then the bottom line. A good number high up doesn't make a winner; the bottom line does.

Stage Metric What it tells you
Attention Hook rate (3-second views ÷ impressions) Did the first frame and first line stop the scroll?
Hold rate (ThruPlays or 15-second views ÷ 3-second views) Did the body keep them watching?
CPM What the audience costs to reach; rising CPMs can sink a good ad
Click Link CTR, cost per click Did the ad make them want to act?
Conversion Cost per landing-page view, cost per quiz start or lead, conversion rate Did the page and funnel match the ad's promise?
Bottom line CAC / CPA, ROAS, then refunds, retention and LTV where tracked Did it make money? This decides the winner

Reading a weak spot:

Pattern Likely problem Try
Low hook rate The opening New hooks or a new first frame on the same body (3a)
Good hook rate, low hold The body after the hook Tighten the body; check the hook-to-body join; Stefan's editing checks
Good hold, low CTR The offer or call to action Strengthen proof, constraints, the offer and the CTA (3b)
Good CTR, low conversion The landing page or funnel Make the page match the ad's promise; check tracking
Converts, but CAC too high Cost to reach, or the audience CPMs, segment fit, a broader or different persona (1c)

Stefan's 5-step diagnosis (for ads worth investigating)

At volume (50+ new ads a month), don't post-mortem every loser. Spend the effort where you had strong conviction, where the angle has worked before or is scaling across the category, or where an execution mistake may have hidden a good angle.

Step Check If it fails
1. Format × audience fit Was the format mismatched with this demographic or offer? (e.g. song ads work in women's health and beauty, but can flop for men who want to get jacked.) If the format is proven for this brand and audience, skip ahead. Test the same concept in a proven format before throwing the angle out
2. Execution (the micro-flaws) The "FU test": does the portrayal of the prospect feel insulting ("that's not me")? Use a relatable regular guy, not a grotesque slob. Overlay overload: winning yappers had 4–5 subtle overlays in the first minute; a loser had 20. Amateur editing: giant or mistimed captions, dizzying zooms, random emojis or sparkles, cheap AI animations. Re-edit or fix the pacing. Auditing the edit before launch can lift win rates 10–15%
3. Angle track record Proven angle that failed now? Likely a weak hook or a fatigued delivery. New angle that failed once or twice? Inconclusive. Angle that has failed repeatedly for months across formats? Probably invalid. Proven: test 4–10 hook variations or another proven format. New: don't kill it yet; change the format, opening situation or lead. Exhausted: kill it, or cap it at 1 in 30 ads
4. Market validation Are competitors scaling this same angle, mechanism or pain point? If yes, the angle is valid; your entry point (hook, lead, spokesperson, offer bridge) is what failed. Iterate the entry, not the idea
5. Portfolio mix Is the batch balanced? Stefan's model: 80% proven, low-variance work; 20% high-variance swings Rebalance. Give weird formats 2–3 tries before moving on if they grab attention. (Our working mix by account stage is in 5a · Strategic bets.)

Before killing an idea, also try it in the other medium (video ↔ static).

Winner iteration: what to test next on a winner

Step 1: preserve the winner. Confirm the business result. Record its full anatomy (2a · Ad anatomy). If only the whole combination is proven, keep the whole combination as the control.

Step 2: choose the branch.

Branch A: how it's shown (keep the message) Branch B: what it says (keep the visuals)
A viral hook or pattern interrupt in front of the winning body (our most common first move) New opening words or copy hook
A new first frame or visual opening A new on-screen headline or text
On-screen text styling or timing Clearer wording of the same message
Delivery or opening audio A new angle or promise
A recut that moves the strongest moment to the front New proof or objection handling
UGC: a new location first, then a new actor A rewritten body section
A new format, conceit or style (CASTS) CTA wording
Static → carousel or motion

If a change touches both branches (a viral opening that changes words and visuals), log it as a combined test.

Step 3: Marcus's priority order

  1. Cheap openings first: a viral hook on the winning body (give a proven body 8–15 hooks before writing a new one); static: same message with a new visual, or same visual with new words.
  2. UGC variations: location first, then actor, same script.
  3. Broader format and messaging development: a new conceit or style, a new angle, a new body section.
  4. Persona and awareness expansion (horizontal): the same message for a new segment or facet, or a new awareness entry point.
  5. Offer or funnel expansion, as a separate experiment.

The rule underneath it all: the biggest expected benefit for the least work.

Step 4: evaluate each variation against its parent under comparable conditions. Better → it becomes the new parent. Worse → keep the control. Inconclusive → name the evidence gap. Better attention alone doesn't make a winner.

Account-wide decline

Check the account and funnel first (tracking, landing page, offer, budget). Then: has the market heard the claim too often (move up a sophistication stage, 1c · Persona bank)? Are the hooks recycled versions of what everyone runs? Is the hook too narrow to reach enough people? Does the first frame look like every other ad in the category?

Keep a log

For every test, record the parent and variant, the hypothesis, the one knob, what stayed fixed, the result and the decision. Over time the account's own hit rate by move (hook swap, style swap, segment transplant, net-new) should replace the default mix. Add every tested hook to the account's hook library.
