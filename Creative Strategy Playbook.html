Creative Strategy Playbook

Choose what to say, how to present it, and which variation to test next. Work from the biggest expected benefit for the least effort, while adapting the approach to the account’s stage.

Start with the Winner System → the one-page loop from research to the next winner.

Research→Persona + awareness + message→Format+Hook→Test + classify→Evaluate + diagnose→Next iteration

What each planning step means

This is a flexible guide for planning an ad. It helps you decide who you are speaking to, what to say, and how to show it. You do not need to start from scratch or follow every step in order each time. When you have a winner, most of these decisions are already made; your next test might change only the hook or format.

Step What it means Moving-company example
Research What have we learned from customers, past winning ads, and competitor ads? Look for problems, desires, objections, and language worth using. Customers complain about surprise fees.
Persona Who are we speaking to, and what situation are they in? Someone comparing moving quotes.
Awareness What do they already understand about their problem and the available solutions? This determines how much explanation the ad needs. They know they need movers and are choosing a company.
Message What is the main thing we want them to understand or believe? Compare what each quote includes, not just the price.
Format How will we present that message—for example, a comparison, testimonial, podcast, or demonstration? Choose an execution from the Format Bank. A side-by-side quote comparison.
Hook What opening words, visuals, or sound will get their attention? Use the Hook Bank to develop the opening. “These moving quotes look $800 apart—until you read this line.”
Test + evaluate Run the ad and assess whether it produces the intended result. What did we learn? Check whether the quote-comparison ad brings in qualified moving inquiries at an acceptable cost.
Diagnose What might explain the result? Use Stefan’s process to check format fit, execution, and testing history before choosing a change. If the quote details are unreadable, test an execution repair before abandoning the message.
Next iteration Based on the result, what should we change next, and what should stay the same? Use the Iteration Map to prioritize the next test. If it works, test another opening while keeping the quote comparison and core message the same.

Illustrative example: The moving-company scenario and $800 difference show how the steps connect. They are not actual customer research, verified quotes, or account test results.

The message is the point you are making. The format is how you present that point. The hook is how you get someone to start paying attention to it. You can keep the same message and test several formats or hooks.

Winner System: the step-by-step loop

Start here. This is the one-page process for producing winners in an account. Each step links to the detailed section. Marcus's October 21 process is the spine; Luke Iha's Copycoders system supplies the vocabulary; the rest of the playbook supplies depth.

Research → persona bank → message → creative combination → write → launch the batch → read results → iterate → feed back.

Rules that make it reliable

  1. Every ad is one cheap swap from something proven (Luke Iha). In order of evidence: our own winner → a competitor's long-running ad → a comment or customer phrase → an organic outlier hook → pure invention. Most of a batch starts from the top of that list; invention gets a deliberate small share. In one of our own lead-gen accounts, 10 of 15 hook swaps on a single proven animated body became winners, and about 25% of variations inside winning families won, against 9–14% in other accounts.

  2. Evidence order: our account's results → category data (long-running ads, Parker, Ad Library run-days) → expert opinion (tier lists, podcasts). Expert rankings disagree with each other and with our accounts; use them to find options to test, never to veto something our data supports.

  3. A winner must clear the bottom line. An ad that wins on a leading metric (CPL, cost per qualified lead, hook rate) but not on CAC or ROAS is a leading winner. Keep funding it to the account's evaluation threshold before building a batch on it.

  4. Fund tests properly. Fewer tests that each reach the account's evaluation threshold beat many starved ones. If a batch can't fund each ad to the threshold, make the batch smaller.

  5. Turn one knob, and make it visible. Name the single change (the CASHED knob) so the result is readable. When the goal is new reach, change visible signals together (actor, setting, first frame). Meta tends to read same-creator, same-room variants as the same ad. Location swaps can improve an ad without reaching new people.

  6. Set the mix by account stage:

    • New account, or one with few winners: mostly net-new concepts, borrowed from the market's long-runners.
    • Some winners with clear gaps: roughly half variations, half new.
    • Mature, with fresh winners: about 70–75% variations and 25–30% new, never zero.

    For a new offer in a category buyers already know, capture existing demand first with solution- and product-aware ads, then move up the funnel.

  7. Loop back. Log which knob produced each winner, bank the winning hooks and bodies, and write repeated corrections into the account's standards.

1. Research where the market is now

Have AI comb Reddit, Facebook and ad comments, Google reviews, news, sales calls, competitor ads, and our own ad numbers and winners. Read the original sources behind every finding.

  • Method and prompt: Marcus's persona-led research process.
  • When the account constraint is unclear, start with D2C Diaries' constraint diagnosis.
  • Mine our winners' comments with the comment goldmine.
  • Sort quotes into angles, benefits, pains, desired outcomes, objections, purchase triggers, misconceptions and stories.

Output: a short read of the market, with quotes and sources.

2. Build the persona bank (one per account / offer)

Define macro and micro personas. For each, record:

  1. awareness level
  2. market sophistication
  3. TAM (estimate with source, date and assumptions, or "unknown")
  4. biggest fears, pains, concerns, desires and motivations for buying
  5. biggest objections

Field definitions: account and offer persona bank. Sophistication: Nick Theriot's stages. Facets (habits, traits, identity, conditions such as "on GLP-1") make useful micro personas; see segments.

3. Choose the message

For the chosen persona, settle the one belief the ad must earn, the mechanism, the proof and the main objection before writing anything: CSI and the 16-Word Sales Letter. Check the P3C2 copy blocks: pain, promise, proof, constraints, curiosity.

4. Match the creative combination to the persona

Pick one entry from each bank and write our adaptation beside it (selector rules, combination card):

Variable What it is Examples Bank
Structure The skeleton: the order of the argument Timeline, story, listicle, comparison Structures and story variations · full list
Conceit What media it pretends to be Podcast, sitcom, infomercial, micro drama Conceit bank
Actor Who we trust, and how they look Monk, skeleton, doctor, spouse, founder Actor bank · Luke's trust ladder
Style The pixels Pixar 3D, claymation, hyper-real, 2D cartoon Style bank
Terrain Where it's shot Kitchen, car, clinic, stage, grocery checkout Terrain and execution bank
Setup Editing and on-screen arrangement B-roll, captions, split screen, props the conceit needs CAST(S)

For a story structure, build the dramatic premise ("Will X solve Y?") first: core wound → dramatic scenario → raise the stakes (in public, at an important event) → how they find the solution → payoff (revenge, glory). See build a dramatic premise and Narrative Ads. Ask what the persona's era cared about (music, media, humor) before choosing the style and audio.

Luke's swap-cost rule: style is the cheapest change; segment is the most expensive.

5. Write and edit

Write the body first, then the hooks.

  • Write about 20 openings, then pick: Luke Iha's hook method, Hook Bank.
  • Edit in three passes: simple words, natural connectors, anti-AI (editing rules).
  • Every claim must be supported, and the hook must pay off in the body.

6. Launch the batch

Label every ad with:

  • Track: variation, repair or net-new
  • What it's one swap from: the winner, swipe, comment or hook it borrows from
  • Knob turned: the one thing that changed (CASHED)
  • Persona and awareness
  • The business result that decides it

Use the next-test brief.

7. Read the results and choose the next test

  • Winner (bottom line confirmed): follow the winner iteration workflow in Marcus's priority order. For video, that means a viral hook first (find one with organic hooks; only ever on a proven body), then other openings, then UGC location before actor, then format, angle, persona and awareness. Give a proven body 8–15 hooks before writing a new one. For tagged combinations, use iterate a tagged combination.
  • Underperformer worth investigating: use Stefan's diagnostic process. Before killing an idea, try it in another format (video ↔ static).
  • Several concepts declining together: check the account and funnel first, then market sophistication and TAM.

8. Feed it back

Add the result to the tested hook and format libraries and the persona bank: which persona, combination and knob won, and on which business metric. After a few batches, the account's own hit rate by knob (hook swap, restaging, persona transplant, net-new) should replace the default mix above.

Research process

For story-led video research, see Harry Delmege and D2C Diaries: find the story and the delivery. It connects account winners, Reddit stories, TikTok delivery references, and Mike Rinard’s trigger moments.

Start here when you need to understand the buyer, find a new angle, or gather the evidence behind an ad. Research should leave you with something specific to say, the buyer’s language for saying it, and proof you can use.

We have several complementary methods. You do not need to complete every method for every ad. A small iteration on a winner can use existing research; a new audience, angle, or offer needs a deeper pass.

Which research process should we use?

Method What it helps you find What you take into the ad
D2C Diaries: Creative Research 101 What is limiting the account, and which research would help resolve it. A prioritized opportunity supported by customer language, with a specific message or presentation to test. See the full process.
CopyHour How competitors sell, what they promise, the claims supporting that promise, and the proof behind those claims. Then examine our own product. A clear promise, supporting benefits, proof, and objections to answer.
Stefan Georgi’s RMBC Who the buyer is, what they have tried, why they think those solutions failed, and how our solution works. Research → Mechanism → Brief → Copy. An explanation and selling argument grounded in the research.
Mike Rinard / StoryMapping The person’s situation, emotions, and real-life moments that bring the problem to the surface. A recognizable scene, story opening, or concrete example rather than a vague pain-point statement.
Account winners and creative references What has worked in our account, and useful messages or presentations elsewhere. A testable creative hypothesis. A competitor’s visible ad is inspiration unless its performance is known.

Source basis: CopyHour notes, Marcus’s compiled Copywriting Research Process, and the Copy Accelerator index of Stefan’s RMBC sessions. This section organizes the written notes into a working process; it is not a transcript of the full RMBC course.

1. Set the research question

Write down the offer, the buyer you are investigating, and what you need to learn. For example: “Why do people hesitate after getting a moving quote?” is a more useful starting point than “Find moving-company hooks.”

For creative references, follow Marcus’s existing order: our account’s historical winners → other ads in the industry → other verticals. Research customers alongside that work. See testing prioritization.

2. Collect customer language and evidence

Where to look What to look for What to save
Sales calls, customer interviews, surveys, support conversations Why they wanted help now, what they tried, objections, and the outcome they wanted. The exact wording, context, and source reference.
Reddit and forums Detailed experiences, replies, recurring frustrations, failed attempts, and disagreements. The compiled notes emphasize replies rather than views alone. The relevant passage and thread link, including context that changes its meaning.
Amazon reviews and product FAQs What buyers praise in five-star reviews, what disappoints them in one-star reviews, questions before purchase, and direct or indirect alternatives. Desired outcomes, disliked tradeoffs, objections, and the language used to describe them.
Facebook groups, pages, and ad comments Questions, arguments, skepticism, frustrations, and reasons people respond. The notes emphasize comments over likes. The comment plus the post or ad it responds to.
YouTube and article comments What people recognize, question, or say is missing from the explanation. Specific language and questions worth answering.
Competitor sales pages, VSLs, and ads Promises, claims, proof, offers, mechanisms, and objections handled. Examples separated from verified facts about our own offer.
Our product, customer results, and supporting research What the product actually does and what supports the proposed claims. Usable proof, its source, and any limits on the claim.

Amazon and Facebook guidance comes from the compiled research-process notes; it is not being attributed to the CopyHour document. Customer beliefs tell us how people think. They do not, on their own, prove that a claim or explanation is true.

3. Use CopyHour to map the selling argument

The CopyHour notes move from market and competitive research into research on your own product:

  1. Study the main direct competitors and their sales material, preferably in the medium you are writing for. The notes suggest starting with the top one to three.
  2. Pull out the promise → supporting claims → proof. Also capture the offer, features and benefits, and objections.
  3. Look at indirect competitors when they help explain what else buyers use to solve the problem.
  4. Go deeper through testimonials, surveys, studies, articles, Reddit, and forums.
  5. Use or study our product. Connect each relevant feature to a buyer benefit and the evidence supporting it.
  6. Compare the research, think, rest, and write. Look for a meaningful gap or an argument we can substantiate.

Output: “Here is what we can promise, why the buyer would care, why they should believe it, and what might stop them.”

4. Use Stefan’s RMBC to understand the buyer and develop the explanation

Use D2C Diaries’ constraint-led research process when you need to decide which audience or problem to investigate first.

RMBC means Research, Mechanism, Brief, Copy. This is Stefan’s process for developing copy before launch. His ad diagnostic process serves a different purpose: evaluating an ad that has already run.

Research: answer the buyer questions

These question labels are preserved from the research framework in Marcus’s compiled notes. Fill them with evidence and buyer language, rather than guesses.

Area Questions from the notes
Customer “Who Is Your Customer?” “How Old Are They Typically?” “What Are Their Hopes and Dreams?” “What Are Their Victories and Failures?”
Beliefs “What Outside Forces Do THEY Believe Have Prevented Their Best Life?” “Sum Up Their Core Beliefs About Life, Love, and Family In 1-3 Sentences.”
Existing solutions “What is the Market Already Using? (List Out)” “What Has Their Experience Been Like?”
Likes and dislikes “What Does the Market Like About Existing Solutions?” “What Does the Market Dislike About Existing Solutions?”
Skepticism “Does the Market Believe Existing Solution Works?” “If Not, Why?”
Curiosity “Has Someone Tried to Solve the Market’s Pain Points Before In A Very Unique Way?” “What Was The Result?”
Perceived changes “Is There A Belief That the Market’s Pain Point Used To Not Exist, Or Used To Not Be So Bad?” “Is There A Belief That It’s Been Recently Exacerbated By Outside Forces?”

The full notes contain further prompts and examples. Use the questions relevant to the offer; investigate surprising explanations before presenting them as facts.

Mechanism: explain why the solution works

Use the research to identify the explanation behind the problem and the solution. What is missing from the alternatives the buyer has tried? What does our offer actually do differently? What evidence supports that explanation?

Do not invent a mechanism to make the copy sound new. The explanation must fit the product and the available evidence.

Brief: organize the argument before writing

Stefan’s brief template asks about the audience, pain points, big promise, existing solutions, new claim or root cause, how the solution works, and the product. It also includes prompts for a paradoxical question, metaphor, bold claim, discovery story, headlines, and upsell.

For an ad, pull forward the relevant parts: buyer → problem/desire → promise → explanation → proof → offer. Use CSI and the 16-Word Sales Letter to clarify the central belief you need to establish.

Copy: write from the brief

Write the argument, then use the hook bank to develop the opening and the format / style bank to decide how to present it. A hook should open the same argument the rest of the ad delivers.

5. Use StoryMapping when you need a recognizable moment

Mike Rinard’s research-to-ad process

Sources: Notes from Mike Rinard Video and ASM Rinard Process, which applies the same framework to a solar audience. Both notes reference this training video. The process below summarizes the written notes; the applied audience sketches are brainstorming examples, not verified findings about every buyer.

Character → emotions → trigger moments → freewrite → Soundbite / Story / Product → revise.

Use freewriting or an interview/group brainstorm. Explore the prompts rather than treating them as boxes to fill in. Start with customer research; if the situations and emotions are hard to describe, go back to calls, reviews, and conversations.

Brainstorm 1: The Character

Think like a detective. Understand who you are selling to and what matters to them in relation to the product.

  • Who are they? Sketch their current situation and the goal they want to achieve.
  • Important people: Who influences their day-to-day life, decisions, and sense of success?
  • Impactful experiences: What has shaped their beliefs, fears, desires, and doubts?
  • Core problem: What problem does the product help solve?
  • Implications: How does leaving that problem unresolved affect their life? Describe the frustrations, roadblocks, and consequences.

Output: A person in a specific situation, with a core problem and a reason it matters.

Brainstorm 2: The Emotions

Think like a psychologist. Connect the problem to the emotions it creates:

Emotion What to explore
Desire What would their ideal life look like if the problem disappeared? What do they most want?
Pain What frustrations, headaches, or obstacles get in the way now? How do these show up in ordinary life?
Fear What are they worried will happen? What worst-case outcome keeps them from acting or keeps them up at night?

The notes distinguish the buyer’s imagined ideal from reality. Use that desire to understand motivation; it is not automatically a promise the product can make.

Output: The driving emotions, each tied to something observable in their life.

Brainstorm 3: Trigger Moments

Find the real-life event that makes the problem and emotion immediate. Brainstorm multiple moments, along with the angles and openings they suggest.

Examples from the notes include a client replying “we need to talk,” losing a client, or noticing difficulty climbing a flight of stairs. The solar application includes receiving an unexpectedly high electric bill or experiencing a power outage. These illustrate the kind of moment to look for; they are not customer testimonials.

Output: A scene someone can recognize—not just a label such as “financial stress.” If convincing moments do not come easily, return to research.

Freewrite, then organize the ad

Write the whole ad without polishing every sentence. Then arrange it into the three-part StoryMap Ad Writing Framework below. The soundbite appears first in the finished ad, but Mike’s notes say to write it last.

Part What it does How to build it
1. Soundbite The punchy opening that earns attention before the reader expands the copy. Bring the reader into the moment. Try dialogue, a question, a little-known fact, a relevant quote, a controversial statement, or a familiar cultural reference. Make clear who or what the opening concerns. Use the hook bank for more opening options.
2. Story Connect a relatable problem to a solution. Establish one goal, one obstacle, and meaningful stakes. Develop the problem/pain story, then the turnaround: how the obstacle is overcome. Introduce the product at the end of the turnaround.
3. Product Turn attention into the next action. Explain what the product is, why it works, what the reader gets, what happens next, and the supporting proof. Give a clear next step.

The notes describe high stakes as something important being lost—potentially professional, emotional, or relational, rather than necessarily literal life or death. Make the consequences concrete and credible for the actual situation.

For the Product section, the notes propose this sequence: initial link for readers already ready → mechanism → link with a simple CTA → what they get/what happens next → link with a simple CTA → additional proof → final, strongest CTA. They also say not every ad needs every element. A higher-commitment action needs more explanation; a lower-commitment next step can need less. This is the source’s long-copy sequence, not a requirement to put four links in every ad format.

Choose a story type

Story type Starting point
Creator Backstory How the creator built the solution, especially when their experience mirrors the buyer’s problem.
Customer Transformation A customer’s movement from the problem to a different outcome, grounded in a real testimonial or case study.
The Nightmare A feared consequence of leaving the problem unresolved, followed by how to avoid it. Keep hypothetical scenes clearly distinct from real customer accounts.

Use the research and story in narrative ads, mini-drama scenes, or another presentation from the format / style bank.

Revise the draft: where is it breaking?

These are Mike’s writing-review checks from the notes, organized into actions. Use them while developing the ad; Stefan’s diagnostic process remains the main post-launch evaluation process.

Check What to look for What to revise
Soundbite Does it lack punch, or attract attention without identifying the relevant person/problem? Strengthen the opening and make the situation more specific.
Story Is it the wrong story, does it start in the wrong place, or is the problem underdeveloped, vague, or unrelatable? Choose a more relevant trigger moment, enter the story closer to it, or clarify the obstacle and stakes.
Product Is the positioning generic, the mechanism missing, or the solution disconnected from the story’s problem? Is it unclear what the product does? Explain the solution clearly and connect it directly to the problem established in the story.
Overall draft Are unnecessary words or details weakening the ad? Are claims supportable and suitable for the placement? Cut distractions and review the actual claims. The old notes’ blanket compliance statements are historical notes, not current platform-policy guidance.

Iterate using the same process

Mike’s notes suggest keeping the Product section consistent while varying the stories and soundbites. That fits our winner workflow: preserve what works and test a new opening or trigger moment first when it offers a useful, low-effort variation. See the iteration map. Keep the product explanation fixed only when it still fits the new story’s problem.

D2C Diaries: Creative Research 101

Source: Marcus’s downloaded recording, Creative-Research-101-SnapYT.App.webm (approximately 65 minutes). This is a paraphrased breakdown of the audio; timestamps are approximate. The scoring cheat sheet and prompts mentioned as on-screen resources were not available in the audio.

Outcome → account constraint → focused research → customer evidence → prioritized opportunity → brief and ad.

See the worked example: from an account constraint to the next ad test for the complete process in practice.

Use this process to decide where to spend research time. It complements Stefan’s per-ad diagnostic process: Stefan evaluates an individual ad; this episode starts with the account’s broader creative needs. It also connects directly to RMBC, which turns buyer research into an explanation, brief, and copy.

Start with what is limiting the account

At 6:07–18:47, the hosts start with the commercial outcome and examine five areas. They score each out of five, but do not give the full scoring rubric in the audio. Use these as questions rather than inventing score thresholds.

Area What to investigate Research direction
Persona coverage Which personas actually attract spend and business results? Is the strategy documented, and is exploration appropriate for the account’s resources? Go deeper into proven people or investigate a genuinely promising new segment.
Messaging diversity Are there distinct angles for each persona? Does the language fit how those people speak? Find specific desires, objections, situations, and phrases. Distinguish the underlying message from its presentation, such as comparison or three reasons why.
Volume and velocity Is the team producing enough useful creative for the account’s spend and testing capacity? Check whether the constraint is production capacity rather than lack of insights. More research alone may not solve it.
Format and vehicle diversity Are useful messages mostly confined to one execution? What relevant presentations have not been tested? Study different ways to present the proven message using the format/style bank.
Concentration and lifespan Does the account depend on a few ads? Are assets lasting less time? Investigate the underlying coverage and creative gaps; concentration alone does not explain the cause.

Their numerical spend and concentration examples are contextual observations, not universal targets for our accounts.

Decide whether to go deeper or broader

Episode example, 19:00–22:38: an account spending about 30,000 per month was testing four personas and launching around 30 net-new ads per month. Two personas attracted 87% of spend; three ads attracted almost 80%. Most spend went through UGC. Their conclusion was to focus on the two proven personas and test a wider range of presentations, rather than spreading production across more personas. The audio does not establish the currency or provide independently verified account results.

This supports Marcus’s winner iteration priorities: develop a working audience/message vertically before automatically expanding horizontally. Keep the winning elements fixed and choose the highest expected benefit for the least work.

When broader research makes sense, 22:45–25:05: they weigh TAM, depth of desire, and domain intuition. In plain language: how many relevant people might want this, how strongly they want the outcome, and what experience with similar markets suggests. An opportunity can exist outside the current account’s history. These signals justify investigation and testing; they do not prove a new persona will convert.

Research existing buyers and people who have not bought

At 25:11–34:02, the hosts distinguish owned customer evidence from prospective or unaware audiences.

  1. Start with existing evidence: reviews, ad/social comments, post-purchase surveys, and customer calls. AI can help organize review themes and unusual cases; read the original context behind the useful findings.
  2. Separate the pain from the purchase trigger: ask why they bought that day. Their example is someone who has disliked their teeth for years but acts because a wedding is approaching. This connects to Mike Rinard’s trigger moments.
  3. For prospective buyers, start with questions around the problem or solution: the hosts use Google Trends and AnswerThePublic to identify directions for investigation.
  4. Take those questions into Reddit, Quora, and other discussions: read the experiences and replies to understand the fear, failed attempts, and language underneath the question. A search phrase identifies a topic; the conversation supplies depth.
  5. Speak directly with people in the audience: ask what they recognize in an ad, what feels wrong, and what questions remain. At 41:05–42:43, one host describes reviewing ads with his mother. That is useful qualitative input, not proof that every member of the audience thinks alike.

Store quotes as building blocks for the ad

At 34:50–36:41, they organize research into the categories below. Retain the source and context alongside each quote. The final column is our practical handoff into the playbook.

Go beneath the surface pain, 36:47–40:55. Feeling tired can mean struggling at work for one person and having no energy for their children for another. Capture the situation and stakes in that person’s words. When expanding to a new persona, changing a label in the hook may not be enough: the story, consequences, and language can need to change too.

Use these findings in the hook bank and research-to-script yapper example. A hook should open the argument the body actually delivers.

Study competitors and organic content with a question in mind

Competitor research, 43:00–50:56: the hosts describe reviewing three direct and at least three indirect competitors. Direct competitors help reveal gaps in the visible creative. Indirect competitors solve the same problem differently and may reveal new language or presentations. Compare the buying journey and price point before transferring an idea. Public ads and comments are references, not access to a competitor’s profitability or complete testing history.

Organize findings into messaging angles, personas, frameworks, and emotions. Record both why this looks useful and what it means we should test.

Mechanism research, 51:20–54:57: investigate why the problem happens, then how the solution addresses it. This is their unique problem mechanism → solution mechanism sequence. Connect it to RMBC’s mechanism work. Verify the explanation and supporting evidence before using it as a claim; neither a customer comment nor an AI-generated explanation establishes that it is true. Simple diagrams or split-screen explanations can make a supported mechanism easier to understand.

Organic research, 55:04–1:03:24: let the audience findings guide searches on TikTok and Pinterest. Study the opening, language, delivery, storytelling, and visual treatment. The hosts highlight affiliate videos and an example of adapting a written “note to self” into video. Add relevant references to the format/style bank and hook bank. Views or engagement can suggest an attention pattern; they do not establish profitable Meta performance. Educational content can suggest questions worth answering without proving a market’s sophistication level.

Prioritize the opportunity and hand it to production

Marcus’s persona-led research process

Source: Marcus’s October 21 masterclass notes, combined with the Suno creative system. This is our proposed account/offer research process, not a claim that every step was taught in the class.

  1. Set the account question first. Use D2C Diaries’ constraint diagnosis to decide whether we need stronger research on an existing buyer/message, a new presentation, or a new audience. For a new account with few winners, retain the broader discovery approach.
  2. Collect evidence with AI assistance. Search Reddit, Facebook/ad comments, Google reviews, relevant news, sales calls, surveys, direct/indirect competitor ads, and our account’s creative results. AI helps search, group themes, and propose hypotheses; read original conversations and inspect actual ads behind the findings. Respect existing access to private calls and customer data.
  3. Separate sources by what they establish. Customer statements show experiences and beliefs; news shows a dated event or claim to verify; competitors show visible messages and executions; our results show outcomes for tested combinations. Competitor visibility does not establish profitability.
  4. Draft macro and micro personas. Group people by shared problem, desired outcome, circumstances, and buying motivation. A micro persona adds a meaningful trigger, role, constraint, or objection—not merely a narrower age band. Check proposed groupings against the source material.
  5. Map awareness and sophistication. What does each group understand about the problem and available solutions? Which promises, mechanisms, and failed alternatives have they encountered? Record evidence and uncertainty. One persona can have several awareness entry points; sophistication is relative to the market and promise, not personal intelligence.
  6. Estimate audience breadth where evidence permits. Define country, eligible population, product relevance, source date, assumptions, and exclusions. Use ranges or “unknown” when appropriate. A broad population is not the same as reachable buyers, and overlapping micro personas must not be added together as separate markets.
  7. Capture emotional and cultural context. Save fears, pains, desires, objections, purchase triggers, natural language, familiar media, humor, and music references. Ask what people actually consume or remember; an era or age alone does not determine taste.
  8. Choose the selling argument before the creative combination. Use RMBC and CSI to establish message, explanation, proof, and offer. Then match a structure, conceit, character, style, terrain, and audio treatment to the person and argument.

Creative Strategy Playbook

New here? Start with the Winner System, the one-page loop from research to the next winner. The Copycoders system (Luke Iha) defines the vocabulary it uses: CASHED knobs, CAST(S), P3C2 copy blocks and segments.

Testing prioritization

Start with the account’s situation. A new account, or an account with very few working creatives, needs big swings and substantial net-new concept testing. Once a winner emerges, prioritize iteration around what is working while continuing to develop new concepts.

Prioritize by leverage

Work from highest leverage to lowest leverage: the biggest expected benefit for the least work. Start with easier tests around what is proven, then work up to tests that require more effort, time, and risk. A bigger potential result does not automatically make a test higher leverage.

For an account with working creative, use this default progression:

  1. Hooks, easy visual format iterations, and messaging tweaks. Build on the winning concept before developing a substantially different argument.
  2. Different angles. Test another reason to care about the same offer. Developing a new angle is usually harder than adjusting a hook or execution.
  3. Proven messaging applied to different personas. Explore whether the message resonates with another audience, adapting it to their situation.
  4. New messaging for different personas. Develop a new argument for that audience when the existing message does not fit.
  5. Different levels of awareness. Change the entry point to meet people at a different stage of understanding. This can begin with the hook; adjust the body when the new entry point requires more context or explanation.
  6. New offers or funnels. Consider larger changes such as a self-liquidating offer (SLO), lead magnet, or another conversion path to capture people at a more top-of-funnel (TOF) level of awareness or reach a bigger share of the market.

This is a default progression, not a rigid ranking of every test. An awareness test that only needs a new hook may belong among the first, easiest tests. Rank the actual work and expected benefit, rather than the category alone.

The new-account exception still applies: when there is little working creative to build on, prioritize finding a viable concept through big swings. That does not automatically mean creating a new offer or funnel.

When to expand the offer or funnel

A new offer, SLO, lead magnet, or another conversion path can capture people at a more top-of-funnel (TOF) level of awareness or reach a bigger share of the market, but it also requires more work, time, and risk. Treat the larger potential upside as a possibility, not a guaranteed result.

The usual priority is to maximize the current direct offer first: fully develop conversion from the existing high-intent audience before adding a funnel for less-aware people. Move toward top-of-funnel expansion when the direct approach has been fully worked through and is limiting further growth. A change in awareness through creative alone can be tested earlier; it does not always require a new funnel.

Finding the first winners

Use this order when looking for creative starting points:

  1. Historical winners in the account. Look first at what has already worked for this offer and audience.
  2. Competitors and the same industry. If the account has no useful history or nothing is working, look at relevant competitors and other accounts in the industry, including accounts we manage. Treat competitor ads as inspiration unless their performance is actually known.
  3. Other verticals. Expand to concepts, messaging approaches, and formats working in other industries or other accounts we manage. Adapt them to the offer and audience.

Research avatars alongside this process: their situations, desires, fears, beliefs, objections, and the language they use. Swipes provide creative references; avatar research helps explain why a concept might resonate and what to change for this audience.

Iterating on a winner

Organize the winner workflow first into vertical scaling and horizontal scaling. Vertical splits into format/style/execution and messaging. Horizontal splits into persona and level of awareness; either can be reached through messaging, execution, or offer/conversion-path changes. Apply our highest-leverage-first priorities across these options. A hook may change messaging, execution, or both.

For video, the default testing order within close iterations is below. Apply the leverage rule to the actual production effort:

  1. Put a viral hook in front of the winning video. This is our most common first video iteration. Keep the winning body and offer, and check that the hook transitions naturally into the ad. Other hook iterations include changing the opening words, first frame, on-screen text, or delivery. Choose one meaningful change, keeping the body and offer consistent; writing a new hook is an option, not a required first step.
  2. UGC variations: test different locations with the same actor and script.
  3. For UGC, test a different actor with the same script, usually after location variations.

For statics, test both directions:

  • Keep the winning messaging and change the visual format.
  • Keep the winning visual format and change the messaging.

These are priorities for building on a winner. A location change can be a useful iteration without necessarily reaching a new audience; assess the outcome rather than assuming each variation creates new reach.

The five awareness stages

Stage Source label What the person knows
1 Problem Unaware Doesn't know they have a problem
2 Problem Aware Knows the problem, unaware of solutions
3 Solution Aware Knows solutions exist, not yours
4 Product Aware Knows your product, not convinced

An avatar is more than demographics: identify their role, situation, frustration, desire, and language. Awareness describes their relationship to this specific problem and offer, not how sophisticated they are generally.

Build and use the matrix

  1. Identify the avatars. Hernan's first prompt proposes 4–6 distinct buyers. Review and shortlist 3–5 against actual customer research, calls, reviews, and account evidence. Treat AI-generated phrases as hypotheses until verified.
  2. Map each avatar across all five stages. His second prompt creates a one-sentence angle for each intersection: the emotional or logical entry point and its framing, not a finished ad. Four avatars × five stages produces 20 candidate intersections.

  5. Connect the angle to execution. Use the Hook Bank for openings and the Format / Style Bank for presentation. Use Narrative Ads when a less-aware audience needs recognition and explanation before the offer.
  6. Record results against the intersection. Track avatar, awareness, angle, hook, format, and business outcome. Use existing account winner criteria, the per-ad checklist, and the Iteration Map to decide what to extend or change.

CSI and the 16-Word Sales Letter: build the selling argument

Use this after research and choosing the persona/awareness level, before writing hooks or choosing an execution. The job is to decide what the buyer needs to believe and why the offer deserves consideration.

Connection: Research process: CopyHour, RMBC, and StoryMapping → customer insight → CSI / central belief → argument and proof → hook and format.

CSI: New Opportunity + Desire + Unique Mechanism

The saved BAD strategy notes describe CSI through these three elements. Their source references are the CSI discussion and the short-form application. The explanations below are our practical synthesis, not a transcript.

Element What to decide Question to answer
New Opportunity The approach or possibility that gives the buyer a reason to reconsider their current way of solving the problem What could they do differently, and why is that relevant to their situation?
Desire The outcome they already want, expressed in concrete terms What do they want to be able to do, change, or avoid?
Unique Mechanism How this approach works and what actually distinguishes it Why could this approach produce the desired outcome, and what supports that explanation?

“Unique” is not permission to invent exclusivity or a proprietary process. Explain the actual difference. A mechanism is how it works; a format is how we present it; a hook is how we open the ad.

Working sentence—not a source quotation: “[Approach] can help [person] achieve [desired outcome] because [supported explanation].”

The 16-Word Sales Letter: one belief, then the argument

Evaldo Albuquerque’s book connects the new opportunity, desire, and mechanism around a central belief. This overlaps CSI; use them together rather than inventing two separate messages.

The saved book study notes cover selected sections of the supplied PDF, including the central-belief framework and the closing questions. The method here is a paraphrased application, not a verbatim reproduction or a claim that the entire book was reviewed.

  1. Name the starting belief. What does this person currently think, based on research?
  2. Name the belief the ad must earn. What would they need to understand or accept to want the next step?
  3. Build the explanation. Connect the opportunity to their desire through the actual mechanism or approach.
  4. Supply proof and answer the main objection. Decide what evidence supports the claim and what could still stop the person from acting.
  5. Lead to the offer and CTA. A short ad needs enough belief for the next click or call; it does not need to complete the entire sale.

Questions to check while writing

These questions paraphrase the book’s buyer-question framework from the saved study notes:

Question What to put in the argument
What is meaningfully different or newly relevant? The opportunity or distinction
Why does that matter to this person? The desired outcome and personal relevance
What evidence supports it? Specific proof for the claim
What has prevented progress? The actual obstacle or limitation of the current approach
What explains that obstacle? A supported explanation, without an invented villain
Why act at this time? A genuine reason to act, if one exists
Why trust this speaker or business? Relevant experience, evidence, or credentials
How does the next step work? The real process and expectations
What exactly should the person do? A clear CTA
What are the costs, effort, risks, and protections? Accurate terms and answers to relevant objections

Use the questions across the ad and destination. They are not ten mandatory beats in every static or short video.

The complete creative workflow

Research → choose the idea → write and produce → test → classify → diagnose → choose the next iteration → feed learning back into research.

Stefan’s framework is our core process for evaluating ads—winners, underperformers, and inconclusive tests. Give every ad a quick structured review; deepen the diagnosis when it can change the next decision. The banks supply creative options; the diagnostic process helps determine which option is worth testing; our leverage rule sets the priority.

1. Research: find a reason to test

Use the Research process for customer-language collection, CopyHour’s promise/claim/proof review, and Stefan’s RMBC research-to-copy sequence.

Start with account winners and their results, then customers’ actual words, objections, desires, and experiences. Look at competitors and the same industry next, then other verticals. Research customer psychology alongside the swipe work. Record the source and distinguish known performance from inspiration.

Output: a specific customer insight, supporting evidence, and a test hypothesis. For example: “People comparing quotes may respond to a clear explanation of what each quote includes.” This is illustrative until customer and account evidence supports it.

2. Strategy: decide who the ad is for and what it should say

Use Hernan’s Avatar Awareness Matrix to map distinct buyers against the five awareness stages and choose the intersection to brief.

Choose the persona, awareness level, and main message. Consider market sophistication: what claims or approaches has this person already encountered? Identify the reason to believe the message and how it connects to the offer.

Output: “For [person in this situation], we will communicate [one main idea], supported by [evidence], leading to [offer and next step].” Nick’s framework helps check relevance, curiosity, implied value, and familiarity with category claims.

3. Write: develop the argument and its opening

Use CSI and the 16-Word Sales Letter to settle the central belief, explanation, and proof before polishing the opening.

Draft the body’s argument: the relevant situation, explanation, supported benefit, proof, offer, and CTA. Use only the elements needed for this ad; a short static does not need a full narrative. For a winning ad, begin with the existing body.

Apply Luke’s method: isolate the core idea, make its framing more interesting, then sharpen the words. Use Dara’s H families and Nick’s N patterns to develop openings. Check that the body pays off each opening. The hook can influence the body and vice versa; this is an iterative writing process.

Output: finished copy, a supported opening, and any alternative hooks with a clear connection to the body.

4. Format and production: make the argument visible

Use the source-bank selector to choose exact structure, conceit, actor and style entries from the supplied banks, with a source/example for each. Select terrain using its explicit provenance: the class defines environment/setup, but no standalone terrain bank was found in the supplied materials. Keep our setting and editing choices separate. Do not substitute generic descriptions and label them Luke’s categories.

Then write our adaptation alongside the copy: why this combination fits the persona and message, the actual scene or demonstration, casting, first frame, spoken opening, text, footage and delivery. For static, specify the headline, image, supporting copy and visual hierarchy. Check message diversity and presentation diversity across the batch; do not force all combinations.

Review execution before launch using Stefan’s questions: does the audience recognize themselves, can they follow the argument, and do the edits or layout help? Check the actual asset, not only the script.

Output: a production-ready brief and finished asset with its message, hook ID, format ID, offer, and test variable recorded.

5. Test: define what we want to learn

If the account has few winners, test distinct net-new concepts. If it has a winner, prioritize meaningful, low-effort variations. Record the parent creative, the hypothesis, what changes, what stays fixed, the relevant business outcome, and the account’s evaluation criteria. Keep combined changes labeled as combined tests.

Output: a test that can inform the next decision, rather than just another ad in the account.

6. Classify: establish what happened

Use the existing reporting setup: BigQuery → Creative Tracker is automated; status classification is partially automated through Claude skills; Meta Testing Dashboard updates are automated through a Claude skill.

Assess the business outcome and evidence quality. Hook rate, clicks, and viewing behavior can help locate questions, but do not replace conversion quality and cost. Missing, insufficient, or unreliable evidence is not a loss or a zero.

Output: an outcome/status supported by the account data, plus any uncertainty. Classification does not explain the cause.

7. Evaluate: use Stefan’s core framework

Use the core ad-evaluation framework to review the result, assess the likely explanation, and choose the next action. Winners need a decision about what to preserve and extend; underperformers need a decision about repair, retesting, or stopping; inconclusive tests need an evidence decision. First check for delivery, tracking, offer, or destination problems that would distort the creative conclusion. Then work through:

Format/audience fit → execution quality → angle/hook track record → credible external evidence → production priority.

Do not deeply diagnose every loser. Do not treat competitor activity as proof or one failed execution as conclusive evidence against the whole idea. Record the observed problem separately from the suspected cause.

CASHED: the knobs on any ad

Use these to name exactly what a variation changes. For a variation, turn one knob and freeze the rest.

A — Angle The psychological frame around the claim Contrarian, story, warning, negative framing ("honestly, don't buy this…")

Which knob changed? Check from the top: a new WHO is D; a changed claim or mechanism is C; the same claim in a new frame is A; the same claim and frame with a new look or presenter is S; only the opening is H; only tone, length or pacing is E.

Decode the winner first (IDENTIFY). Before turning a knob, write down what the winner is currently set to: desire and avatar (D), problem framing, mechanism and proof (C), emotion (E), frame (A).

Three tracks for a winner: net-new (a new core idea, which keeps the account from fatiguing), variations (one knob), and scaling (change nothing). Luke's starting budget split is about 70% scaling, 20% iterations, 10% net-new. Production effort shifts with account maturity: about 85% net-new for a cold account, falling to about 25–30% for a mature one, and never zero.

P3C2: the copy blocks

Pain · Promise · Proof · Constraints · Curiosity, plus Conditions (offer terms). Most copy feedback is one of two moves: add a missing block, or strengthen a weak one.

  • Pain: lived symptoms, sequenced from general to specific, then cinematic, then emotional, then the core wound.
  • Promise: generous, anchored to the one core transformation, at the edge of believability.
  • Proof: right after each promise. Never make the claim bigger than the proof. Experiential proof (demos, tests, comparisons) is strongest; psychological proof (logic, explanation, candor) is the most underused.
  • Constraints: money, time, effort, and identity conflicts. Acknowledge the belief, wedge in a counterexample, then elaborate the new option.
  • Curiosity / mechanism: the new, simple way from pain to promise, ideally with an evocative name.
  • Strengthen any block with CRAVES: Clear, Relevant, Accurate (the market's exact words), Visual, Expressive, Specific.
  • Persuasion equation (a scoring lens, not math): value = promise × (proof × curiosity) ÷ constraints × conditions.

Segments: a spine with facets

  • Outcome is the spine (the desire or pain), cut narrower beneath it.
  • Facets hang off it: demographic (what they are), identity (who they say they are), condition (a fact that limits fit), value (what they prefer).
  • Awareness sits above the map, not inside it, so you don't count one slice five times.
  • Shade in the real ads; empty, evidenced cells are the gaps. Rank gaps by size, urgency, market size, what competitors run, and the moment.
  • Horizontal-scaling tests: a facet must pass a one-second "is this me?" test; a phrase found in many ads but no customer's mouth is a symptom line, not a segment; don't go horizontal while two segments are still printing.

Hooks and editing

  • Hooks: put the most interesting thing first. Make it relevant within 10–20 words, open a loop, and use concrete, charged words. Lead with the consequence, not the mechanism. Avoid the pain-first default every competitor writes. Write about 20, then pick. Luke's "vicious" bar always stops at the client's compliance line.

  • Three editing passes:

    1. Simplify: Germanic words, 3rd–5th grade.
    2. Natural language: connectors such as and, but, so, because.
    3. Anti-AI: no staccato fragment stacks, no announcement phrases, no tidy triplets, no "not X, it's Y" tic.

    When the same correction comes up twice, write it into the account's standards.

Identify what is working before iterating

Start with the business result, then compare the finished creative with its parent and related tests. Do not start by choosing an attractive hook template.

  1. Verify the winner: inspect qualified conversions, cost/return, and the account’s evaluation criteria. Confirm comparable attribution, timing, offer, audience, and delivery conditions.
  2. Open the parent/control beside the winning asset. List exactly what changed: hook, body, message, format, actor, setting, offer, or several elements.
  3. Check related test history. Same body with different hooks can support an opening hypothesis. The same message succeeding across different formats can support a message hypothesis. The same format succeeding with different messages can support a format hypothesis. These comparisons are stronger when conditions are comparable; they are not automatic causal proof.
  4. Use attention metrics to locate questions. Hook rate, retention, and clicks help explain behavior, but better attention with worse qualified conversion performance does not establish a better ad. No single metric maps uniquely to a creative fault.
  5. Write the supported conclusion and its limit. “This combination won” is valid when only one execution worked. “This opening appears stronger with the same body” requires a relevant comparison. Keep the winning combination intact as the control while investigating.

Concrete record: parent ID → variant ID; what changed; observed result and source; what that supports; what remains uncertain; next test. For example: “The headline changed; the visual and offer stayed fixed; qualified-inquiry cost improved in comparable conditions. Retain this version and test a second headline. 1. The Core Problem & Reframing

When spending at scale ($1M+/month), closing the feedback loop is not merely looking at in-platform metrics (ad spend, ROAS, CPA, conversion rates) to determine whether an ad won or lost. Identifying the outcome is easy.

The true challenge—and the core of closing the loop—is diagnosing why an ad won or lost so you can validate, iterate, or discard the underlying hypothesis.

The Operational Reality Check

At high production volume (e.g., 50+ new creatives per month), do not waste time conducting forensic post-mortems on every single loser. Focus your diagnostic energy where:

  • You had strong strategic hypothesis conviction.

  • An angle has worked historically or is scaling across the category.

  • Execution or editing errors may have masked an otherwise viable angle.

Step 1: Format & Audience/Demographic Congruence Check

Start at the macro level before blaming the hook or copy: Was the format fundamentally mismatched with the target demographic or offer?

  • Rule out proven formats quickly: If the format has consistently converted for this brand and audience (e.g., Yapper style for female demographics), do not waste time questioning the format—move directly to execution and angle.

  • Look for demographic incongruence: Certain formats carry inherent demographic biases.

  • Example: Song ads might convert well in women's health and beauty categories, but flop when targeted at men who want to get jacked, or a younger and more AI-savvy demo.

  • If internal account data or broader market observation shows a format skews heavily toward one demo, testing it against an opposing demo introduces format friction.

  • Diagnostic Action: If format mismatch is suspected, test the same concept in a proven format before throwing out the angle hypothesis.

Step 2: Creative Execution & Production Diagnostics ("The Micro-Flaws")

A winning angle will fail if the visual execution breaks buyer psychology or pacing. Auditing video editing before launching can improve ad win rates by 10–15%.

Check the edit against three critical failure points:

1. The "FU Test" (Prospect Depiction)

  • How is the prospect portrayed on screen?

  • If the portrayal feels derogatory, caricature-like, or overly insulting, prospects experience an immediate visceral rejection: "Fuck you, you think that's what I look/act like? That's not me."

  • Example: In a men's testosterone ad, portraying the protagonist as a grotesque, super-schlubby slob before taking the supplement triggers defensiveness and kills conversion. Portraying a relatable, regular guy with a dad bod preserves empathy and identification.

2. Pacing & Overlay Overload

  • More graphics and visual stimulation do not equal higher retention or conversion.

  • Case Study: In a high-converting Yapper ad campaign, the scaled winners averaged 4 to 5 subtle image overlays over the speaker during the first minute. An underperforming variant had 20 image overlays in that same first minute—turning a personal, connective conversation into a frantic slideshow and destroying the viewer-speaker bond that makes Yappers convert.

3. Amateur Video Editing Artifacts

  • Watch for distracting, low-intent editing choices made by offshore teams lacking direct-response advertising instincts:

  • Giant, obnoxious, or mis-timed captions.

  • Nauseating zoom-ins, rapid camera shakes, or disorienting cuts.

  • Out-of-place visual clutter (e.g., random dancing emojis, sparkles, or cheap AI animations).

Historical Parallel: In the long-form VSL era, offers often flopped because of bad spokesperson delivery or poor video production—not bad copy. In short-form video ads, the creative strategist must actively police editing choices.

Step 3: Angle & Hook Diagnostics (Hypothesis Viability)

Once format and production execution are ruled out, evaluate the core messaging hypothesis against its testing history:

Angle Category Historical Context Diagnostic Rule Action to Take
Proven Angle Has converted previously in this account Likely a weak hook variation, fatigued delivery, or bad lead Test 4–10 micro-hook variations or test across alternate proven formats.
Novel / New Angle Tested only 1–2 times and failed Inconclusive — 1 to 2 failures do not provide sufficient statistical or creative data Do not kill prematurely. If you have conviction, change the format, the opening situation, or the lead copy.
Exhausted Angle Tested repeatedly over several months across multiple formats/hooks with zero traction Hypothesis is likely invalid for this account/audience Kill the angle entirely, or cap it strictly at 1 in 30 creatives per batch to avoid wasting production bandwidth.

Step 4: Market Validation & External Benchmarking

When an internal test fails, check external market intelligence:

  • Are competitors or category leaders scaling this same angle?

  • If multiple brands in the vertical are successfully spending behind the same mechanism, pain point, or angle, the underlying hypothesis is validated by the market.

  • An internal flop does not mean the idea doesn't resonate; it means your hook framing, lead-in, spokesperson, or offer bridge didn't land. Iterate on the entry point rather than abandoning the angle.

Step 5: Portfolio Allocation (The 80/20 Creative Mix)

To prevent feedback loop analysis from stalling creative momentum, structure monthly production using an 80/20 portfolio model:

  • 80% Proven / Bread-and-Butter (Low Variance):

  • Built on established formats, validated angles, and proven pacing (e.g., 33–35 out of 50 ads are Yapper style).

  • Drives consistent ROAS, reliable scaling, and predictable cash flow.

  • 20% High-Variance / Experimental Swings (High Upside):

  • Wild creative swings, novel formats, and disruptive visual concepts (e.g., AI sitcoms, David Fincher / Molly's Game freeze-frame dramatic montages, AI escalation ads).

  • Rule for weird ads: Expect low win rates, but do not kill an experimental category after two duds if there is strong attention-capturing rationale. Run small batches (2–3 iterations) to see if you can crack the format before moving on.

Addendum: Big Idea vs. Mechanism vs. Concept vs. Angle vs. Hook

When marketers and creative strategists talk about creative testing, these terms get thrown around interchangeably. Here is the dead-simple way to separate them:

1. The Big Idea

  • What it is: An idea that is instantly comprehended as exciting, important, and beneficial—leading to an inevitable conclusion that makes it easy to sell the product. The best Big Ideas have a "meme factor" or dinner-table sharability: you can explain it to someone in 10 seconds, they immediately grasp it, and they want to share it with someone else.

  • The Role: Anchors the entire promotion and funnel. If the prospect accepts the Big Idea, buying the product is the only logical next step.

  • Examples:

  • Pimsleur: "To learn a new language as an adult, you need to mimic how a child learns."

  • Skincare: "The biggest cause of premature wrinkles isn't age or genetics—it's washing your face in the bathroom sink."

2. The Mechanism

  • What it is: The scientific, biological, or technical how that proves why the Big Idea is true. It satisfies the logical, skeptical mind.

  • The Role: Provides proof and credibility in the body of the VSL, sales page, or advertorial.

  • Examples:

  • Pimsleur: Dr. Paul Pimsleur’s graduated interval recall memory method.

  • Skincare: Chloramine oxidizers in municipal tap water stripping the skin's protective lipid barrier.

3. The Concept

  • What it is: The packaging, format, or storytelling vehicle you use to dramatize that Big Idea in front-end ads.

  • The Role: You can test 5 to 10 completely different concepts to deliver the exact same Big Idea.

  • Examples (for the Skincare Big Idea):

  • Concept A: A candid, raw iPhone confession from an everyday 50-year-old woman.

  • Concept B: An investigative lab experiment testing beakers of sink water under a microscope.

  • Concept C: An "insider whistleblower" aesthetician exposing what luxury spas never tell clients.

4. The Angle (The Specific Lens / Villain / Symptom)

  • What it is: The specific entry point of attack inside a concept. It’s the exact villain you blame, the symptom you isolate, or the demographic/situational lens you look through.

  • The Role: Multiple angles can live inside one concept without changing the production format.

  • Examples (inside Concept A — everyday woman confession):

  • Angle 1 (The Villain): The chlorine and chemicals added to city tap water.

  • Angle 2 (The Hidden Symptom): Why expensive moisturizers "sting" or pill up right after washing.

  • Angle 3 (The Regional Trigger): Why women in hard-water states (Florida, Texas, Arizona) age noticeably faster.

5. The Hook (The First 3–5 Seconds)

  • What it is: The actual thumb-stopper in the feed across spoken words, visual action, and on-screen text.

  • The Role: It does not sell the product or explain the mechanism; its sole job is to stop the scroll and buy the next 10 seconds of attention.

  • Examples (for Angle 3 — regional hard water):

  • Hook 1: "If you live in Florida and wash your face in the bathroom sink, you need to hear this before tonight..."

  • Hook 2: (Holding up a cloudy glass of faucet water to the camera): "See that crust around your bathroom faucet? That’s what was sinking into my crow's feet for 10 years."

  • Hook 3: "I’m 52, and the biggest mistake I made with my skin wasn't the sun—it was the water in my sink."

Build a dramatic premise before writing lyrics

Use this with the narrative workflow and existing dramatic-short brief. The class’s core-wound discussion begins around 36:31; its comparison of story variants is around 52:56–54:53.

  1. Core wound: what does the practical problem mean emotionally to this person? Examples include feeling unwanted, unreliable, excluded, or unable to be the parent they want to be. Validate relevance through research rather than assigning an insecurity to everyone.
  2. Dramatic scenario: choose a concrete event that reveals it through action. Marcus’s relationship brainstorm—flirting, rejection, a rude remark, a discovery, or embarrassment—offers different scenes for a shared emotional concern, not seven separate personas.
  3. Raise the stakes: ask what could be lost, who witnesses it, why this moment matters, and whether a meaningful deadline exists. More explicit or shocking is not automatically more relevant.
  4. Dramatic question: will this person overcome this obstacle before the consequential moment? This should create a question the ad actually answers.
  5. Discovery: how does the solution enter naturally? A friend, research, experience, or qualified authority can supply the turn. Connect the product’s real mechanism and proof here.
  6. Payoff: resolve the opening concern. A second-chance scene can mirror the beginning; a run-in can show the payoff first and explain how it happened afterward.

A fictional scene can illustrate a problem without masquerading as a customer testimonial. The offer must support the product outcome implied by the ending. Dramatic revenge, reconciliation, or admiration is not a guaranteed product benefit.

Harry’s process: a relatable person becomes a convert

Harry distinguishes an expert, a friend, and a convert. His focus is the convert: someone goes through an experience, changes their belief about a product, and tells a friend why. The experience gives the ad a story and a reason for the recommendation. 00:56–03:31

He explicitly says he does not use one rigid body framework. Treat the following as ingredients to arrange around the story, rather than mandatory beats at fixed timestamps.

Research: find the story and the delivery

Harry searches Reddit for stories and TikTok for stories plus how people tell them. Broader communities about relevant life situations can provide more material than product reviews alone. His example is a dating community: the life event supplies the story, while the product must have a believable role in it. 06:00–09:28

He considers three things:

  • Believability: could the audience recognize this situation as something that happens to them or someone they know?
  • Intrigue: is the story interesting enough to watch? A more sensational story can earn engagement without producing the strongest sales result.
  • Existing response: do comments, upvotes, or views suggest people care about the story? These are research signals, not proof that an ad adaptation will convert.

D2C Diaries adds a starting point: look at the account’s recent winners, identify the persona and angle, and turn those into research keywords. Their example uses a 90-day review and leading spenders; use our account’s actual winner classification rather than spend alone. Then read Reddit, forums, Quora, and ad comments manually to uncover the language and lived consequences. Episode: 12:34–16:17.

Research output: the situation, the customer’s words, the emotional consequence, the story source, the relevant product truth, and a reference for natural delivery. Distinguish real customer testimony from an illustrative or dramatized story; adapting a stranger’s story does not make it the creator’s own experience.

This extends Mike Rinard’s character → emotions → trigger moments. A surface problem becomes a concrete moment in someone’s life.

Script the argument; leave room for performance

Harry prefers a complete script over vague bullet points: the writer develops the argument and the creator makes it believable. Leave room for personality, natural movements, and small delivery details. 03:34–05:57

D2C Diaries offers a useful test rather than a single rule: record one scripted take and one take in the creator’s own words. Keep the essential claims and story intact, then compare the executions. Pitch, tone, pauses, and pacing matter; read the script aloud to hear whether a person would actually say it. Episode: 34:16–42:55.

Hooks: visual, spoken, and on-screen text

Harry’s visual examples are small, natural actions: bringing hands together, adjusting hair, closing a car door, gesturing, or propping up the phone. His spoken and text hooks fall into three groups. 09:37–12:47

Hook direction What it does What to test
Curiosity + benefit Gives the viewer a question and a reason the answer matters. A specific change or discovery connected to a supportable benefit.
Broad reach Creates interest without narrowly qualifying the viewer at the opening. A broad mistake, surprise, or story premise; verify that attention turns into useful response.
Audience qualification Makes the intended viewer recognizable immediately. A relevant persona, situation, or problem callout using customer language.

In Harry’s account example, the broad hook had the higher hook rate, while curiosity plus benefit produced stronger spend with a higher-intent audience. Do not choose a winner on hook rate alone. Use the Hook Bank for more patterns and the per-ad checklist to evaluate the outcome.

D2C Diaries adds question openings, skepticism, failed solutions, and a surprising explanation of the problem. Use the ones that naturally lead into the story; avoid adding curiosity that the ad never resolves. Episode: 16:18–16:45, 20:15–23:40.

The oracle: bridge from story to solution

The oracle is the person who introduces the solution to the narrator. It may be an expert, friend, or aspirational peer—not necessarily a doctor. Harry uses this to make discovery part of the story instead of abruptly saying the narrator found a product online. 13:43–15:25

D2C Diaries expands the choice: who would this buyer trust, admire, or listen to in this particular situation? The answer comes from the persona’s relationships and motivations. Episode: 17:04–20:07.

The oracle is a storytelling role, not a way around proof. Do not invent an endorsement or treat a claim as substantiated because someone in the story says it.

Mechanism, alternatives, and objections

Explain how the product creates the desired outcome, why relevant alternatives may fall short, and answer concerns when the explanation raises them. The oracle can help explain the mechanism. 15:27–17:24

D2C Diaries emphasizes weaving failed attempts and skepticism into the narrative rather than interrupting it with a list of objections. Translate the mechanism into words the narrator would use; do not suddenly switch to technical product-page copy. Episode: 20:15–22:36, 40:45–42:04.

Build the argument first using CSI / the 16-Word Sales Letter and RMBC. Product and comparison claims must match the available evidence.

Journey, product introduction, and CTA

Where continued use matters, Harry likes a timeline that makes the experience and expectations understandable. The progression should reflect actual evidence, not an invented result schedule. Finish with the real offer, relevant guarantee, and a clear next action, delivered in the narrator’s voice. 17:27–19:53

There is no mandatory runtime or product-introduction timestamp. Harry describes winners of different lengths and introduces products at different points. Cut unnecessary setup and weave the product into the story when it fits; do not stop a story halfway through to recite benefits. 20:02–21:48

D2C Diaries: keeping the story engaging

Move from the pain point to its consequence

Research what the problem changes in daily life: work, confidence, family, relationships, or social participation. The ending should resolve the same emotional thread established at the beginning. This is the episode’s connection to research and narrative, rather than merely choosing stronger adjectives. Episode: 23:48–28:07.

Track curiosity through the whole script

The hosts describe a loop ledger: at each stage, know what the viewer is waiting to learn, where the answer arrives, and what keeps them interested next. This includes the transition into the solution, where scripts can become a flat sales pitch. Episode: 30:03–34:07.

Use this practical worksheet (our adaptation of that discussion):

Story beat Question opened Payoff Bridge to the next beat
Opening moment What happened, and why does it matter to this person? Establish the situation and stakes. Introduce the obstacle or failed attempt.
Discovery What did someone notice or suggest? Explain the relevant insight. Raise the narrator’s natural doubt.
Trying the solution Why might this work when alternatives did not? Mechanism, evidence, or demonstration. Show what happened next.

Use timelines, genuine surprises, a demonstration, or a relevant objection to maintain interest. Each opening needs a payoff. Pauses and natural interruptions can change pacing, but should not pad the script or substitute for the argument.

Worked example: from moving-quote research to a winning yapper

1. Read the research and find the moment

Suppose we are researching people who have moving quotes but have not chosen a company. Following the Research process, we collect the customer’s words and their context before writing hooks.

Illustrative research input What it suggests Creative decision
Sales-call note: “One quote looks cheaper, but I can’t tell what’s actually included.” Comparing totals does not resolve the decision. Explain how to compare what is included, rather than lead with a discount.
Forum comment: “I spent the whole evening with these quotes open and still couldn’t pick one.” The problem creates a recognizable moment of frustration. Open with two quotes on the kitchen table.
Ad comment: “What happens if there are stairs or the move takes longer?” Uncertainty about conditions is an objection. Show where those conditions appear in a written quote.
Interview note: “My sister told me to ask what could change the price.” Advice from a trusted person can advance the story. The sister is the oracle; she introduces the question and, in this fictional story, the company she used.

Insight: The buyer is not only looking for a low number. They want to understand what they are agreeing to before moving day. This is a hypothesis to validate across the research, not a conclusion justified by four isolated comments.

4. Complete illustrative script

Performance direction: Talk as if explaining the experience to a friend. Start with two quote pages in hand. Leave room for natural pauses, but keep the product explanation understandable. Any documents filmed must be approved examples with personal details removed.

I had two moving quotes on my kitchen table, and I still couldn’t tell which one was the better deal.

I kept looking at the totals. One was lower, so I thought, okay, that’s probably the one.

Then my sister asked, “Are they actually quoting for the same move?”

And I was like… it’s the same house. What do you mean?

She meant the details. The stairs. What I needed moved. What would happen if something changed.

So I went back through them. One explained those things. With the other, I still had questions.

She sent me the company she’d used, and I asked them that exact question: what’s included, and what could change the price?

They checked what I was moving and the access at both places, then sent it back in writing. Here’s the work they included. And here’s the part about changes.

It wasn’t “nothing can ever change.” I wouldn’t expect that if I added a room full of furniture. I just wanted to know what we were agreeing to.

That was the bit I’d been missing. I was comparing two totals before I’d compared the actual job.

If you’re at that stage, ask for an itemized quote. You can request one below, and check the details before you decide.

Before production: quick review

  • Can we identify the persona, real-life moment, and reason to care?
  • Does the story lead naturally to the product and explain why it works?
  • Are claims, comparisons, testimonials, and endorsements supported?
  • Does each curiosity loop get an answer, including through the product section?
  • Would this creator actually say these words? Read the draft aloud after a break and cut unnecessary setup.
  • Does the ending resolve the opening problem and give a clear next step?
  • Have we specified what the test changes and what stays fixed?

Winning Ad Styles — the list

A style is the filter the ad is rendered through. Same script, same claims, same offer, same structure, different picture: Pixar, camcorder, Notes app, courtroom sketch. Nothing the buyer has to believe changes when you swap it, which is why it's the cheapest swap there is. You are not writing a new ad. You are re-rendering a proven one.

The one-second test: could you re-render the exact same script in this and change nothing but the picture? Yes → style. No, the script has to change shape → that's a conceit (workflow 3). Entries marked (both) are honestly both.

Three rules. 1. Match the skin to the awareness: cold traffic wants native, ugly, low-fidelity (camcorder, Notes app, raw UGC); product-aware traffic can carry polish. 2. Style breaks are a tool: cutting from cartoon to real footage at the product reveal says "the story part is over, this part is real." 3. Some styles do the targeting for you: a reggae track, a country kitchen, an anime skin pick the audience before a word lands.

How to use this tonight

  1. Take a winner, or anything with real signal. Freeze the script.
  2. Pick five styles from the list your account has never run.
  3. Brief each one: same words, same order, same offer; only the rendering changes. Note where a style break belongs, if anywhere.
  4. Render and launch. Every one is one swap from proven, so you know what the test measures.
  5. A style wins → it's the next winner in; pick five more.

Viral hook in front of a winning video

Our most common first video iteration: put a viral hook in front of a winning video. This can be an existing opening asset or an adaptation of a viral opening; it does not require writing an entirely new hook.

  1. Pick the winning video as the control.
  2. Select a viral hook that can lead naturally into its message. To find one, use Luke Iha's organic-hooks method: a burner feed trained on the market, reels at 5–20× their creator's normal views, and the spoken, on-screen and caption hooks pulled word for word. Only swap the subject word; don't "improve" a proven hook.
  3. Put the hook in front and retain the winning body and offer. Watch the join for a confusing jump or repeated introduction; record any transition edit.
  4. Record the source hook, exact opening used, parent creative, and what changed.
  5. Compare business performance with the original. Viral performance is a reason to try the hook, not proof that it will improve this ad.

Hooks span both winner-iteration branches: a visual/audio pattern interrupt, first frame, or delivery change is format/style/execution; new opening words or headline wording is messaging. A viral opening that changes both is a combined hook variation. See the two-branch workflow.

Nick Theriot: research-led hook development

Source: Nick Theriot (@nicktheriot_), “Read this if you want to write better ad hooks in 2026,” dated September 23 in the text Marcus supplied (saved source text). This section summarizes the supplied article; its spending figures, performance examples, and explanations of platform or brain behavior have not been independently verified. The templates below are our adaptations, not quotations or tested winners.

Use this framework before selecting a hook template. Its useful contribution is to connect a specific customer insight, familiar category claims, an opening worth watching, and a relevant visual.

From research to a testable opening

  1. Choose an entry point from research: a desire, symptom, or known problem. Save the actual customer language and its source—reviews, interviews, comments, or surveys. Separate customer evidence from competitor ad copy and our own hypotheses.
  2. Check three things: will the intended buyer recognize the situation; is there a relevant question or reason to keep watching; and is the value of watching apparent? These are writing prompts, not a guarantee of scale. A direct benefit or offer can still be a useful hook.
  3. Consider market sophistication: what promises, approaches, and mechanisms has this audience already encountered? Decide whether the opening needs clearer proof, a meaningful difference, or recognition of a familiar frustration.
  4. Check audience fit and breadth: does the opening address enough relevant people for this account’s geography, offer, and growth objective? Broadening it should preserve buying relevance. Do not apply the article’s 100,000-person cutoff as a universal rule or infer spend potential from audience size alone.
  5. Plan the whole opening: words, first frame, on-screen text, sound, and delivery. Choose a relevant visual from the Format Bank. An unusual visual should help the message land; novelty alone is not the goal.
  6. Develop variations from the insight: AI can help organize research and draft alternatives, but each variant must remain tied to the source insight and supported offer. Keep the body fixed for hook-only tests and check the transition.

For statics, the headline and image perform the opening’s job together. A headline can also be a hook; the article’s headline-versus-hook distinction is a writing lens, not a strict classification.

Awareness versus market sophistication

Awareness asks what this person understands about the problem, solutions, and your offer. Market sophistication asks what claims and approaches they have already heard, tried, or become skeptical of. Someone can understand the category well and still be unfamiliar with your product.

Nick’s five stages, in his own words from the article you supplied:

Stage Nick’s exact wording
Stage 1 “You're first to the market. You can make a basic claim ("How to get rid of acne") and it works because nobody has heard it before.”
Stage 2 “You're second or third to the market. You have to exaggerate the claim ("How to get rid of acne in 7 days") to stand out.”
Stage 3 “The market is crowded. You have to introduce a new mechanism ("Viral new laser treatment vanishes acne in 30 minutes") because the buyer has already tried the obvious solutions.”
Stage 4 “The mechanism itself is getting copied. Now you have to exaggerate the mechanism ("The 30-minute laser treatment already used by 10,000 women to vanish acne") to stand out again.”
Stage 5 “The market is completely burned. Every claim sounds like a lie. Now you have to identify with the buyer's frustration ("What nobody tells you about acne treatments when you've already tried 5 of them") because they've heard every claim under the sun and stopped believing any of them.”

Separate application note: the examples above are Nick’s illustrations, not verified treatment claims or approved ad copy. Use the stages to ask what buyers have already heard or tried. Nick uses the word “exaggerate”; our copy still needs supported claims. A mechanism is the explanation of how the solution works.

Account-wide decline: TAM and market sophistication

Nick Theriot covered both TAM and market sophistication. Below are his questions and instructions, quoted from the article Marcus supplied, followed by short application notes. The account-level routing is our addition, not a diagnostic tree from Nick.

First: is other stuff still working?

Our routing: if one ad is weak while other ads still work, use Stefan’s diagnosis. If several previously working ads are declining, review Nick’s questions below. If only one format or audience group is declining, focus there first.

Separate account check—not Nick’s wording: did the landing page, offer, tracking, budget, or sales process change when results dropped? For example, if the form stopped working, fix the form. That result would not tell us that buyers are tired of the message. A decline across one account also does not, by itself, prove a market-wide trend.

1. Has the market heard this claim too many times?

“Match the hook to the sophistication stage of your market.”

“If you're running a stage 1 hook in a stage 4 market, no golden hook formula will save you.”

Use Nick’s five stages above. Look at the promises competitors repeat and what customers say they have already tried. If a basic promise feels familiar, his next directions are a new mechanism, a stronger mechanism presentation, or identifying with the buyer’s frustration—depending on the stage.

What to test: a different explanation of how the actual offer works, evidence supporting that explanation, or an opening about the frustration buyers express after trying other solutions. Hernan’s matrix is for audience and awareness expansion; it is not the market-sophistication framework.

2. Is the hook just another copy of what buyers have already seen?

“does the hook you launched this week actually create novelty in your buyer's brain, or is it just another recycled version of what already worked for someone else?”

“Pick your point of entry from research.”

Nick’s three starting points are:

  • “A desire the buyer already has”
  • “A symptom the buyer is currently experiencing”
  • “A problem the buyer knows they have”

What to test: an opening built from a specific customer insight, using reviews, comments, interviews, or surveys. Use Nick’s hook patterns to express that insight.

3. Is the hook speaking to enough relevant people?

“Check TAM size.”

“Before you launch a hook, ask yourself: how many people in the country would this hook stop the scroll for?”

TAM means total addressable market. Here, Nick is asking whether the hook is so narrow that few potential buyers would care about it.

Nick’s next instruction:

“If the answer is under 100,000, rewrite the hook to appeal to a bigger audience.”

What to test: “rewrite the hook to appeal to a bigger audience.” That is the change Nick recommends here.

Our application note: his 100,000-person cutoff is a source recommendation, not a universal pass/fail rule. The broader hook still needs to attract people the actual offer can serve.

4. Does the opening look like every other ad in the category?

“Do something visually different in the first 3 seconds.”

“The hook isn't just the text. It's the whole first 3 seconds of the ad.”

His examples are “A weird angle. A prop nobody uses in your category. An unexpected setting. An animation style your competitors haven't tried.”

What to test: a new first frame, setting, prop, delivery, or visual treatment from the Format / Style Bank. Keep the message and body fixed when testing only the visual opening.

5. Does the hook give the right buyer a reason to keep watching?

Nick’s “golden hook formula”:

  • “Calls out the ideal customer (so they stop the scroll)”
  • “Creates curiosity (so they watch more)”
  • “Implies a benefit (so they feel like watching is worth their time)”

What to test: make the buyer relevance, unanswered question, or value of watching clearer. The ad body must deliver what the hook sets up.

Choose the next test using the Iteration Map. If the audience needs a story to understand the problem and offer, use Narrative Ads. Nick’s performance and brain-response claims remain his claims; these questions guide tests rather than establish the cause of a decline.

Example: turn a research question into a hook test

Hypothesis to validate: people comparing moving quotes struggle to tell whether the totals cover the same work. If customer research supports that experience, test N05: “Why do two quotes for the same move include different things?”

Pair it with F04 — Comparison and footage showing the actual differences. The intended buyer recognizes the situation, the question creates a relevant reason to watch, and the implied benefit is a clearer comparison. The body must explain the differences and connect naturally to requesting a quote.

To test the copy opening, retain the winning comparison footage, body, offer, and CTA. To test a new visual opening, make that a separate branch. Follow the Iteration Map for the next decision.

What we adopt: research specificity, relevant curiosity, sophistication, and deliberate visual choices. What remains source opinion or unverified: universal claims that only certain hooks scale, fixed attention timings and dopamine explanations, promised spend outcomes, and blanket claims that AI cannot originate useful ideas. Account conversion results determine what becomes a winner in this bank.

Luke Iha: strengthen the idea before polishing the hook

Use the workshop notes as a writing method across the existing H, L, and N entries. It does not require another set of overlapping hook IDs.

  1. Isolate one idea. Write what you are actually trying to say in plain language. Start from the ad body or a raw concept.

Ways to make the idea more interesting

These are development moves, not additional hook families. Use them to improve a hook from any bank.

Move What to explore Evidence boundary in our playbook
Reverse the expected value Could something seen as good have a drawback, or something dismissed have a useful benefit? Explain a real tradeoff; do not manufacture harm.
Increase the stakes What meaningful consequence sits behind an apparently minor problem? Develop a supported consequence, rather than converting “ineffective” into “dangerous” without evidence.
Create contrast Put two relevant, unexpectedly different ideas together. The contrast must still lead toward the product’s argument.
Reverse causation Question whether the assumed cause and effect might run the other way. A causal reversal is a research question until established; do not present it as fact for surprise.
Add a useful distinction Show that a familiar category contains meaningfully different types. Use real distinctions; do not invent diagnoses or arbitrary subtypes.
Create a characterization Give an existing experience or thing a vivid, memorable description. Make clear when it is a metaphor or nickname, not an official category or proprietary invention.
Use the “tightrope effect” An unexpected statement makes the viewer want to see how it can be explained. The body must deliver an honest payoff. A misleading implication is not repaired by a technically true explanation later.

Key passages: 0:09:38–0:13:30. Luke explicitly says he is not telling people to lie (0:07:55), while encouraging exploratory brainstorming before editing. Our application keeps imaginative drafts separate from claims ready to use.

Make the language do more work

  • Evoke the feeling rather than just naming it. Concrete actions and sensory details can carry more emotional force than labels such as “devastating.” Luke’s central distinction is showing versus telling (0:13:40–0:20:40).
  • Try one to three relevant trigger words. They should sharpen the actual idea, rather than decorate a generic sentence. This complements the Dara buzzword reference; a word list does not replace a concrete scene.
  • Put the strongest material early. Move the heart of the idea or the most arresting phrase toward the beginning; remove throat-clearing.
  • Use specificity selectively. Luke suggests roughly one number and one proper noun as a starting heuristic, then gives exceptions. The point is to avoid crowding the opening, not enforce a word-count rule.
  • Use active language and simple phrasing. Prefer words the audience immediately understands. His discussion of word origins is a language heuristic, not a requirement to classify every word.
  • Add conflict, stakes, or a characterization when they help. Keep one dominant idea instead of piling multiple unrelated surprises into the hook.
  • Use copy blocks sparingly. The transcript names pain, curiosity/mechanism, proof, promise, constraints, and conditions. That passage is brief; it does not provide full definitions of every block.

Narrative Ads: Writing for Unaware and Less-Aware Audiences

When to use a narrative

Starting awareness Where the story starts What the ad needs to establish
Unaware A recognizable situation, desire, symptom, or event, without assuming the viewer has named the problem Why this moment matters → what explains it → why the offer is relevant
Problem-aware A frustration, personal cost, or previous attempt the viewer already recognizes A useful explanation or new way forward → proof → offer
Solution-aware or more aware The experience of choosing, comparing, or using a solution Relevant differences, objections, evidence, and a clear next step

Narrative is especially useful to explore when reaching less-aware audiences, but it is not limited to them. An unaware audience does not automatically require a long ad or a different offer. Use the amount of story needed to earn understanding and the next action.

  1. Strategy: choose the persona, starting awareness, customer insight, and supported message before selecting the story.
  2. Format / Style Bank: choose Story/Narrative as the structure, then choose the presentation and visual style separately. A story can be narrated, acted, delivered as UGC, or written as primary text.
  3. Hook Bank: choose an opening that introduces the story's actual tension. The body must pay off that opening.
  4. Narrative workflow below: plan the reader, stakes, explanation, cast, turning point, proof, and ending; map beats before drafting.
  5. Iteration Map: use this as an option for awareness expansion. A full new story is a larger test than changing an opening; preserve Marcus's highest-leverage-first priorities.
  6. Per-ad checklist: evaluate the result and choose the next test. For a winning narrative, try an opening variation first when appropriate, preserving the body, message, and offer.

Example: entering through an everyday moment

Illustrative moving-service concept—not a customer testimonial. The supported message is that comparing quotes means checking what is included, not just the total.

  • Unaware entry: a household has two moving quotes open on the kitchen table and cannot see why the prices differ.
  • Recognition: show the confusion through the scene; do not assume they already know which quote details matter.
  • Discovery: compare real inclusions and terms from approved example quotes.
  • Offer bridge: explain the actual quote-comparison service and its role.
  • Next step: invite the viewer to request information or a quote. Return to the table with clearer comparison questions, without inventing savings or a completed move.

A problem-aware version could begin directly with “Why do these moving quotes include different things?” The core message can stay the same while the entry point changes. Choose the presentation in the Format Bank and develop alternative openings in the Hook Bank.

Decide the story in this order

  1. Reader and buyer. Identify their situation, what they value about themselves, what they have tried and who actually makes the decision. A partner may be the buyer and a useful narrator; do not assume that relationship without evidence.
  2. Personal stakes. Name what the problem interrupts in their life. Move from an abstract problem to a particular moment: a task left unfinished, a conversation avoided or a plan that becomes harder.
  3. Supported explanation and offer. Establish what the service or product actually changes and why that matters. Do not invent a hidden cause because the reference has one. Explain a documented process when that is the honest reason to consider the offer.
  4. Cast. Choose who experiences the problem, who tells the story and who introduces the useful information. These can be the same person.
  5. Opening and turning point. Pick an event that makes the stakes visible. Plan how discovery changes the person's understanding or next action.
  6. Proof and ending. Identify evidence, objections, actual offer terms and the action the audience should take. Choose an early detail that can return meaningfully at the end.

The workshop distinguishes the order of these decisions from the order of the finished copy. Starting with a shocking opening before knowing what it sells can produce a story with no credible bridge to the offer.

Techniques worth carrying into our ads

An event-led opening. Start at the moment something happens, then supply only the history needed to understand it. A second sentence should develop the tension or change its meaning. The promise of the opening must connect to what the offer can actually do.

Small event, meaningful stakes. The useful lesson is specificity, not escalating every story into tragedy. Show what the person does, avoids, notices or says. Research supports the experience; the script supplies the original presentation.

Backstory that earns its space. Remove the history and ask whether the turning point still matters. If it does, shorten the history. If the history creates the stakes, retain it and make its important details pay off later.

A credible source of information. The workshop distinguishes lived experience, self-taught research, professional experience, a chance encounter and a loved one's research. The explanation should match the speaker's actual knowledge. Do not invent expertise or imply that a paid spokesperson is an independent customer.

An explanation the audience can picture. A short analogy can clarify a supported process. It must not replace proof or create a false claim. A service can explain practical choices without borrowing a supplement ad's hidden biological cause.

Buying criteria before a brand preference. Show what matters in choosing a solution, then explain which verified features of the offer meet those criteria. Compare alternatives fairly; do not fabricate a failed competitor purchase or pretend only our offer can qualify.

Payoffs planned in advance. Revisit an object, place, task or phrase introduced earlier. The workshop's dress story returns to the fitting and photographs; the trucker story returns to the route; the prison story returns to a dance and household repair list. Borrow this technique, not those stories or their claimed results.

Proof in observable terms. Where real evidence exists, make the change understandable through actions and life moments. Timelines, numbers, quotations, third-party reactions and guarantees all require support. A staged scene is not a verified testimonial.

Minimum volume to judge Threshold before calling win/loss 3–5 sales = winner, OR 3–10 booked calls = winner
Winner tiers (advanced) How much spend one creative can sustain Tier 1 ≈ 5 sales · Tier 2 ≈ 20 · Tier 3 = breakout

Tier 2 / Tier 3 winners are the true breakouts — replicate those hardest. A Tier 1 may have just hit a niche pocket and died.

Part 2 — How Much Creative You Actually Need (the volume math)

Pull a 30–90 day window (max 6 months — older data drifts with algorithm/offer changes). For video and static separately, pull: # launched, # winners, total winner spend.

Metric Formula Why it matters
Win rate winners ÷ launched How effective your creative is. ~10%+ is a healthy floor for individual creatives. Doubling it roughly halves the volume you need.
Avg spend per winner winner spend ÷ winners How much spend one winner sustains before fatigue. The single biggest lever on total volume. $3k = tiny market/mistargeting; $50k = healthy.
Total winners needed (spend gap ÷ avg spend per winner) × safety margin Spend gap = target monthly spend − current. Safety margin 10–20%.
Creatives to produce winners needed ÷ win rate Split by your video/static mix (e.g. 80/20).

🟡 Worked example. Going $150k → $1M/mo. Win rate 15%, avg spend/winner $50k → ~15 new video winners → ~100 videos total. Same scenario but avg spend/winner only $10k → hundreds of creatives. Takeaway: if the number looks insane, your spend-per-winner is usually the culprit (too-niche targeting or media-buying issue), not your output capacity.

Part 3 — Iteration Prioritization Model

Higher = easier to produce & more likely to win (closer to proven winner). Lower = more net-new effort. But see the similarity caveat after the table.

# Iteration Effort Meta-similarity verdict
1 Same script/avatar/idea, new location Lowest Weak signal — likely same signature
2 Same script/idea, different avatar Low Strong — new human = new signature
3 Same script, different visual hook (first 3–5s) Low Moderate
4 ~Same script, different pattern interrupt (1–3s bolt-on) Low Weak–moderate
5 Different lead/hook, same body & core idea Medium Moderate
6 Same lead & core idea, different body Medium Moderate
7 Different hook & body, same core idea (e.g. talking-head → podcast) Highest Strong — borders net-new

Iterations beat net-new on win rate on average — but if you only iterate you run out of angles and the account collapses. Keep farming net-new concepts to find Tier 2/3 breakouts. It's a balance.

🔴 Similarity caveat (Meta's view). Iterations #1, #3, #4 change execution, not the creative's DNA. Meta's Andromeda reads a "visual signature" (talent, setting, camera/angle, message, emotion) — not the file. Same creator + same setting + different script = usually NOT enough differentiation; it's served, the viewer thinks "seen it," and scrolls. To reliably register as a distinct asset, change multiple signals at once: different talent, different environment, different format/angle, different core insight.

Gem Ranking — picks the right ad for the right person at the right moment The discerning reader

Implications: Creative is the targeting now (flipped from pre-iOS14). Practice radical simplification of account structure — fewer campaigns/ad sets, let the algorithm find audiences. Your job is to feed it creative fuel, not build manual targeting layers.

🟡 Strategic volume, not raw volume. Meta + agency A/B test: 25 variants beat 5 variants → +171 purchases, 59k fewer people reached, 50% cheaper cost/conversion. But volume only works through the persona lens. Teams that blindly pushed to 4,000–5,000 assets/mo saw reach drop and CAC rise. Think "flavors": 10× the same flavor = same audience; varied flavors = signals to find new audiences.

Part 5 — Personas: the real diversity engine

Stop targeting demographics ("male, 25–35, likes football, UK"). Target psychographics — motivators & barriers.

  • Macro persona → broad bucket (two people can share it).
  • Micro persona → primary desire, core fear, core belief. This governs the message.

Example (SpaceGoods, wellness): "High achiever" → message = productivity/scaling. "Stressed worker" → message = peaceful mind/wellness. Same product, totally different creative — and you discover audiences you didn't know you had.

🟢 The framework (Meta's recommended matrix): 3–5 personas × 3–5 angles/insights each × multiple formats (static / video / carousel) = a diverse creative portfolio without realizing you've made dozens of ads — all built consumer-first.

Mindset shifts

  • Kill the winner/loser mentality → build a creative portfolio: ~80–85% proven themes, 15–20% calculated risk (new themes/formats). Label each asset: framework (proven), big swing, or calculated risk.
  • Watch for survivorship bias — also ask "what are we not doing / who are we not talking to?" (e.g. over-indexed on 40+, ignoring under-40 with product appeal = untapped spend).
  • Research is the 80/20 of creative. Review mining, surveys, trending organic. Find how customers describe the problem (≠ how the brand describes it). Refresh quarterly, not once a year.
  • When you find winning messaging in a persona, that's gold/insight — test it across 5–7 different vehicles (soulmate theory: everyone consumes info differently).
  • Diversity must not compromise quality. Track hit rate; it should stay flat or improve as volume rises. If hit rate drops as you add volume, the system is broken.

Part 5b — The Diversity Sliding Scale & the Persona "Cake"

Source 3's practical framing of what "diverse enough" actually means.

The sliding scale (left = wasted spend, right = incremental reach)

Position What it is Verdict
Left Similar message + similar visual style (same angle, same look, same creator/hook) Least differentiated — collapses to one signature
Middle Differentiated message, same style (new script/angle/persona, but same format & look) Partial
Right Differentiated message + differentiated style Sweet spot — drives incremental new reach. Invest here.

🟡 Real proof point: one of their biggest clients went from $54k → $622k/mo spend (same Oct window, YoY, >10×) by obsessing over persona + creative diversity to generate signals. Separately, Meta showed two different UGC creators, both in a kitchen, similar message — and Andromeda scored them as the same asset. Same-setting/same-message = wasted creative budget.

The persona "cake" (layered multiplication)

Build creative from the bottom up; each layer multiplies the one above:

  1. Product (base)
  2. Persona — the most potent fuel (e.g. ~12 personas for a broad product)
  3. Angle — pain points / emotional narrative (~20 per persona → ~500 angles)
  4. Vehicle / style — podcast, VSL, founder story, supermarket walk-through, etc.
  5. Tip: font / color / copy — minimal signal impact (copy is a conversion lever checked after the hook, not an attention/signal lever)

Do the math and you have ~50,000 possible concepts — so "what more can we do?" is never the real constraint; prioritization is. Run this research/insight build on a ~2-week rhythm.

🟢 Workstream split: when an angle hits, fork into two streams — (a) reskin/visually diversify the proven angle, and (b) keep an always-on hunt for the next big persona/angle unlock (these have lower hit rate but drive the step-changes). "Different motivators unlock new audiences ~89% of the time."

Part 5c — KPIs for Incremental Reach (the daily scoreboard)

Creative diversity should be measured, ideally owned by a creative strategist as a secondary KPI checked ~daily / pulled monthly.

KPI What it tells you Benchmark / note
Incremental reach New audience you're reaching over time Ask reps for the report. As % it falls with scale — fine as long as absolute volume rises.
CPM-R (cost per 1,000 accounts reached / cost of incremental reach) Proxy when you can't get rep reports = CPM × frequency. Spikes down after a diversity sprint, levels as it saturates. ~10–20% sweet spot relative to brand size; <10% = faster diminishing returns. Red flag: CPM-R rising faster than spend. Top unlock assets can be 5–6× lower CPM than account avg.
New visitor % Whether the creative actually pulls new people to site (not just engagement) Declining new-visitor % strongly correlates with declining performance. Lagging indicator of rising CPM-R.
Hit rate % of assets that win Must stay flat/improving as volume rises.

Mental model: Signal engineering — signal-rich ads are the context, ad spend is the prompt. The algorithm shifted deterministic → probabilistic (if X then maybe Y based on signals). Signals = watch time, hook rate, engagement, conversion — Meta latches onto who's resonating and finds lookalike behavior.

Part 6 — Partnership Ads (high priority)

  • Top brands run 30–50% of spend on partnership ads. Consistently lower CPA, higher CTR.
  • They inject authenticity + a completely different signal set (creator's handle, community, content style) → helps reach new audiences. A multiplier of diversity.
  • Biggest mistake: siloing them into their own campaign/ad set. Incorporate them inside your core campaigns (radical simplification) so signals lift the whole campaign.
  • Getting started: founder/internal-team stories work; use Creator Marketplace (nano → large, with performance filters). New feature: search your own brand's followers for active creators.
  • Treat it as always-on collaboration, not one-off. Give creators real creative freedom; not every creator should/will work with you (authenticity is the point).
  • Tactic that's working now (Source 3): pair the partnership ad with a native Instagram static that looks like a story (like an influencer story) — driving very cheap CPMs, high CTR, and lifted conversion rate. Note: in their data partnership ads lifted lower-funnel conversion more than incremental reach (hypothesis: they convert the most stubborn, previously-unconverted audience pockets).

Open question from the source: Whether re-running a top in-account creative as a whitelisted partnership ad gets penalized for similarity — Maxim wasn't sure; he's "seen it work." Test cautiously.

Part 7 — AI Tools

  • Use the full Advantage+ creative suite (text generation, video editing, image-to-video) — no longer just for early adopters. Text-gen can build persona-aware copy if it knows your brand DNA.
  • Caution: don't go on autopilot (random "Happy Birthday"/holiday music misfires). Always keep a human in the loop for brand tone/voice.
  • AI = augments creativity, doesn't replace it. "Creative will be automated; creativity never will." Largest spenders skew authentic/organic, but the best brands also use AI as its own vehicle (AI voiceover, winning message → 3rd-person AI framework).

Part 8 — Measurement

  • One in-platform metric isn't enough. Build a holistic measurement framework across paid + organic + email + offline + marketplaces.
  • Build the measurement process early — retrofitting incrementality testing onto a complex media mix is painful.
  • Incrementality is the north star. Invite creative strategists into measurement conversations so creative + data work toward one goal (no silos).

Part 9 — Three New In-Platform Creative Reports (rolling out now)

Announced by a Meta employee ("Jason") on X. These surface inside the ad account / Ads Manager under Analyze & Report → Ads Reporting → Account Insights. Rollout is staggered — not in every account yet.

Report What it shows How to use it
Creative Fatigue Report % fatigue score at the asset level — when a target audience is over-saturated and interaction is dropping Leading indicator of CPA rise. If a winner ticks toward 30–40%, inject volume / accelerate the next sprint. Less critical if an ASC already holds many (e.g. up to 50) top performers to absorb spend.
Creative Similarity Tells you directly when Andromeda sees two assets as the same — surfaces the similarity scoring from the Meta workshops inside the account Audit for assets impeding new reach / grouped to the same audience. 🟡 Brand-protective = harder to scale. A highly consistent brand look reads as high similarity, which now works against incremental reach. The fix isn't less brand — it's wrapping your value proposition / brand feeling around more visual + message diversity (and leaning on vehicles + high production). Even AG1 has added noticeable color/visual diversity vs. their older library.

Part 10 — Quick-Fire Tactics (Q4 / always-on)

  • Horizontal + vertical scale (surface area): each sprint, promote winners into the core campaign AND into a separate manual/bid-cap campaign on a weekly cycle. Time it just before the weekend to ride high estimated action rates — weekends compound.
  • Break out ASCs by persona once the diverse creative process is working — over-consolidated structures at scale limit diversity delivery.
  • Migrate proven winners into a bid cap under the new ASC algorithm (esp. if on older pre-ASC setups).
  • Stop turning ads off in non-testing/scaling campaigns: with mass diversity + many high performers, trust Meta to reallocate; spend trends back and forth and the long tail picks up as Andromeda improves. (Caveat: only safe when you have many trusted winners, not 3 fragile ones.)
  • More consolidation at the testing/ad-set level and let it sit ≥1 week — new campaigns/ad sets are taking longer to learn than ~6 months ago.
  • High production is the fastest-growing % of spend and over-indexes on incremental reach (like problem-aware/unaware ads). If it failed before, it's usually weak persona/angle/script — not the format.
  • Concept-level variation now needs bigger swings: change the full visual journey (car → first-person → UGC → supermarket), reframe (e.g. talk to the 45+ target while showing a younger presenter), or reframe the benefit psychologically (loss- vs gain-framing). Creator-only diversity is delivering less lift than ~6 months ago unless creators differ in gender/age/motivation.
  • AI production (2026): Sora 2 (faster, ~½ cost, strong UGC realism) + Veo 3 + nano-banana "remixing" pushing toward >50% of creative being heavily AI-driven. Use as its own vehicle, human-in-the-loop.

One-line summary (Meta strategist's parting advice)

🟢 "Embrace creative diversity with a strategic framework. Stop thinking winning/losing ads — build a creative portfolio from a deep understanding of personas, their motivators and barriers. Build a matrix of creative concepts, feed the algorithm rich mixed creative, and let it reward you."
