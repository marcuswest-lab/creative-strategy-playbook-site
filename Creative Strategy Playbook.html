What the job is

Make winning ads (and funnels). In practice that means:

  • Write ads that resonate with the audience, and make them different enough that the algorithm serves them to different people.
  • Make them fun, creative, persuasive, and aimed at the right people.
  • Know the market deeply enough to talk the way they talk.
  • Keep up with winning formats and styles, and apply them to the brand.
  • Watch for patterns in winners and for gaps nobody has filled.
  • Place strategic bets: variations vs net-new, proven vs wildcard, which sources.
  • Write strong hooks and know direct-response fundamentals well enough to execute.

Messaging comes first; the look is chosen right after the format (it limits what the script can say, especially in video) and made in production. Step 2 decides which ads (the whole batch); step 3 plans each one.

The shortcut: a restyle or new format on a proven winner already has its message and script. Pick the new look (3d), skip the writing, and go straight to production. That's why it's the cheapest move.

Marcus's rule: personas first, creative second. Use the research to define the personas (awareness, sophistication, TAM, fears, desires, objections) before choosing structure, conceit, actor, style, terrain or setup. The creative combination is chosen to fit a persona, never the other way round.

Why the grammar matters:

  • "Format" hides what's actually changing. "Timeline ad" names the structure; "podcast ad" the conceit; "monk ad" the actor; "Pixar song ad" the style × conceit; "skeleton timeline ad" the actor × structure.
  • Elements stack: a timeline, inside a podcast, delivered by a skeleton monk, in Pixar style, sung.
  • It makes patterns visible to you and to AI: tag ads by these elements and you can see which one is working.
  • It makes variations easy: keep the element that's winning, change the rest (step 2).

Luke's decision stack: where creative sits

From his weekly "certainty calls": every result comes from seven layers, top to bottom. When ads fail, the cause can sit above the creative.

  1. You: who you are, what you want and don't want
  2. Value: value creation and value capture
  3. Targeting: offer, market, avatar
  4. Messaging: positioning, mechanism, big idea
  5. Assets: hooks, beats, copy blocks, micro-scripts, characterizations
  6. Creative: the ad, VSL, image, email
  7. Optimization: copy-chiefing, CRO, testing

His recurring lessons from those calls: market intimacy (read comments, listen to sales calls, talk to customers); make it visual (people give half their attention; show the doctor, play up the relationship); know which pain you're talking to (every pain has its own shape); soften the copy and tell stories; check the reading level (lower it); and one thing, not three.

Our account rules (Marcus)

Luke teaches the method. These rules come from our own accounts and sit on top of it:

  1. Every ad is one cheap swap from something proven. In order of evidence: our winner → a competitor's long-running ad → a comment → an organic outlier hook → pure invention.
  2. Evidence order: our account's results → category data (Parker, Ad Library run-days) → expert opinion.
  3. A winner must clear the bottom line (CAC or ROAS), not just a leading metric.
  4. Fund tests properly. Fewer tests that reach the account's spend threshold beat many starved ones.
  5. Turn one knob, and name it, so the result is readable.
  6. Account-specific overrides live with the account, for example ED-CREATIVE-NOTES.md in the ED project.

Taste and instinct

Your long-term goal is a sixth sense for what will work: it operates below language because you notice patterns too subtle to explain (Luke cites chicken sexers and Blink). With AI producing unlimited options, this judgment is the job, which Luke calls being "paid to feel."

How to train it:

  • Saturate your brain in the account's winners and in audience language, repeatedly. Copy top ads into a running doc and read them.
  • Feel it in your body (interoception). Read two headlines and notice which one lifts something in your chest or gut. Notice how you get sold to.
  • Gray vs color. In any ad, mark what's genuinely interesting versus boilerplate. The colored parts are the seeds for new ads.
  • Study everything: tabloids, black-hat direct response, big brands, brain rot. Anywhere humans pay attention.

The research process

Do it at onboarding, then again every few weeks. Read the sources yourself first, then have AI process everything; each catches what the other misses.

Start from what the account needs: a stronger message for current buyers, a new presentation, or a new audience. "Why do people hesitate after seeing the price?" beats "find hooks."

Step 4: answer the buyer questions (Stefan Georgi's RMBC)

Area Questions
Customer Who are they? How old? Hopes and dreams? Victories and failures?
Beliefs What outside forces do they believe kept them from their best life? Their core beliefs in one to three sentences?
Existing solutions What are they already using? What has that been like? What do they like and dislike about it?
Skepticism Do they believe existing solutions work? If not, why?
Curiosity Has someone tried to solve this in a very unusual way? What happened?
Perceived change Do they believe the problem used to not exist, or not be so bad? That outside forces made it worse?

Then RMBC continues: Mechanism (what the alternatives are missing and what ours does differently) → Brief (buyer → problem → promise → explanation → proof → offer) → Copy.

Step 5: find the trigger moments (Mike Rinard)

Character → emotions → trigger moments. Who are they, and who matters to them? What do they desire, what pains them, what do they fear? What real-life moment brings it to the surface (a client saying "we need to talk," an unexpected bill, struggling on the stairs)? A trigger moment is a scene the viewer recognizes, not a label like "financial stress." These become story openings.

Step 6: map the worldview

What does the segment already believe, who are its enemies, and which debates is it in? (See 1b · Persona bank.) Unaware ads are built on this.

Output: the persona bank (1b · Persona bank) plus a research doc of quotes sorted into the buckets above.

The AI research pipeline (Luke's brain dump, worked on a detox drop)

  1. Product page analysis: product, core problem as the brand frames it, audience, features and claims, proof, price and offer, tone, and one "brand belief" paragraph.
  2. Market verbatims: exact quotes with source and context, about 8 each for fears, frustrations, wants, beliefs, and what they've tried.
  3. Psychographic mapping: group the quotes into four themes (identity, problems, dreams, obstacles), each split into named sub-groups (e.g. "the embarrassed self", "the frustrated seeker"), then write one key roadblock statement.
  4. Solution mapping: a solution statement plus a feature → why it exists (the obstacle it removes) → practical benefit → emotional benefit → core desire ladder.
  5. Competitive intelligence: the main competitor's claims, targeting, angle and proof, price, what customers like, complain about and why they stopped, then the gap.
  6. Customer personas: short day-in-the-life portraits (age, job, family, routine, the moment the problem bites), then big ideas built from them (see 3a).

Organic: a burner feed for viral hooks

Make a separate Instagram or TikTok account and train its algorithm to match the prospect: follow the right accounts, watch their most popular videos to the end, open the comments. Then look for outliers: videos doing many times their creator's normal views. Swipe the hook, connect it to your argument, and read those comments too.

1b. The persona bank: who, awareness and sophistication

Sources: creative strategy in 15 minutes, 16 god-tier formats (facets), unaware ads masterclass, 7 hidden sources for statics (the 5×5 matrix), a## Luke's 3D persona model (ODF): Outcome × Demographic × Facet

Horizontal scaling = targeting more personas: new slices of the market, or narrower ones inside a slice, so ads get better delivery and higher conversion. Vertical scaling = more ad formats for the same persona.

Caveat first. You can overdo personas. Many top ads, especially top-of-funnel, unaware story ads, are broad and target no single pain. But very specific personas also produce breakthrough winners, especially in bigger accounts and for middle-of-funnel, problem-aware ads. Use the model to spot big untargeted slices; don't try to test every sub-sub-segment.

Two definitions:

  • Segment: a slice of the market (men over 35, Black women, vegetarians, people who want more libido).
  • Persona: a name or identity for that slice, so it's easy to talk about and write to (Diabetes Dave, Low Libido Lou, Smoker Sam, Cortisol Cara). Alliteration is optional.

The three dimensions (Luke's testosterone example):

Dimension What it is Types Examples
Outcomes The pains and promises: what they want, or want gone Outcome → sub-outcomes Testosterone (low T · optimize T) · Weight loss (moobs · love handles · puffy face) · Sexual vitality (erections · libido) · Energy (brain fog / focus · afternoon crash) · Physical performance (gym · recovery)
Demographics Who they are Buyer · gender · age · race · identity Self / wife / partner · men / women · 20s, 40s, 60s+ · Black, White, Asian, Latino · married, single, dad, retired
Facets Something true about them, where much of the opportunity is State · Occasion · Value · Tribe · Beliefs On finasteride, smoker, on TRT · just divorced, just turned 50 · no prescription, no TRT · faith-based, biohacker, gym-goer · cortisol, estrogen, microplastics

State facets matter most: habits and conditions that are true about them (cigarette or weed smokers, vegetarians, on GLP-1, diabetic, on TRT).

Stack them (that's why it's 3D). Multiply as few or as many dimensions as the account can support:

  • Black men (demographic) struggling with weak erections (sub-outcome)
  • Women buying gifts for their husband (demographic only)
  • Men who want to lose weight (outcome only)
  • Older men (demographic) who want to lose moobs (sub-outcome) and are chronic smokers (facet)

How to fill the grid, in order:

  1. Give AI the model (the transcript above) and let it draft the outcomes, demographics and facets for your product; then adjust with judgment.
  2. Scrape competitor ad transcripts: who is each ad targeting?
  3. Scrape comments on your ads and competitors' ads.
  4. Scrape Amazon reviews of competitor products (Apify) to see which outcomes and sub-outcomes people actually have.
  5. Trial and error, plus common sense.

Choosing which cells to test: go where there's signal (ads, organic content, comments, forums, research) and ask: how big is this market, is it worth the time, and is a little risk worth it to find out? Facets and specific demographics like race are usually underused.

You click cells until "everyone" is cut down to one person you can write for, name the persona, save it to "Personas to target", then ask the writing bots for ideas for that persona.

Awareness: where the ad starts the conversation

Level They know… The ad's job
Unaware Nothing about the problem, or they aren't thinking about it Capture attention with high curiosity, then make them care
Problem aware They have a problem, not that solutions exist Articulate the problem sharply, then introduce the solution type
Solution aware Solutions exist, not yours Position your product type as the best option
Product aware Your product, but not convinced Build value and differentiate
Most aware Your product; they need the offer Offer, price, urgency

For writing, Luke lumps these into two buckets: unaware + problem aware (curiosity-led, native-looking, more variety, scales further) and solution + product aware (product-centred, proven templates). Unaware ads are the hardest to write and the biggest pool of buyers. See the unaware hook types.

The diversity matrix: segments × awareness levels. Five segments × five levels = 25 buckets. True creative diversity means covering many buckets, not clustering in the obvious ones.

Market sophistication: what they've already heard

Awareness is what they know. Sophistication is how many claims they've already seen (Eugene Schwartz's stages, as summarized by Nick Theriot):

Stage The market What works
1 First to market A simple claim
2 Second or third A bigger claim
3 Crowded; obvious solutions tried A new mechanism
4 Mechanisms copied An enlarged or proven mechanism
5 Burned out; every claim sounds like a lie Identify with their frustration; insight and story

Worldview: the layer under the segment

Unaware ads in particular work by speaking to the segment's worldview: what they already believe, who their enemies are, which debates they're in. Luke's frame (after Blair Warren) is that people respond to those who encourage their dreams, justify their failures, allay their fears, confirm their suspicions and help them throw rocks at their enemies. Also: give them a mission, an identity to be proud of, permission to want, and clarity when things feel uncertain.

The persona bank (one per account / offer)

Build macro personas and the micro personas inside them. For each, record:

  1. Segment (outcome × demographic × facet)
  2. Awareness level
  3. Market sophistication
  4. TAM: an estimate with source, date and assumptions, or "unknown"
  5. Biggest fears, pains, concerns, desires and motivations for buying
  6. Biggest objections
  7. Worldview: beliefs, enemies, debates

Overlapping personas: never add their TAMs together. Example: the ED persona bank in the ED project (research/ed-persona-bank-v2-2026-10-22/).

2. Decide the batch: which ads to make, and why

Sources: what "strategy" means, the "boring" ad moves behind $100mm+, creative strategy in 15 minutes.

Ideas aren't the problem; choosing what to test first is the most important part of the job. Strategy is choice: deciding what not to do, with limited production and limited spend. This step works at the account level: it ends with a list of slots for the week. Step 3 then designs each slot.

The whole job, at its simplest: look at what's working in your account, competitors' accounts and the wider market, do more of it, and fill in what's missing.

Luke's process in one line: know the working elements (the decisions below) → find openings with gaps, patterns and taste → turn them into hypotheses → build the production plan.

Make the decisions conscious. You already make thousands of unconscious decisions when you write ads; most make sense, some don't, and you can't tell which while they stay unconscious. There's no single best strategy. Luke's aim is one that's coherent and legible: you can read it, explain it to a client, and adjust it.

The decisions you're making (make them consciously)

  • Iterate on a winner, or make a net-new ad?
  • Which sources do I pull from?
  • More proven, or more wildcard?
  • New segments, or deeper into one segment?
  • New styles, formats or conceits?
  • What mix of awareness levels?

Answer them with patterns + gaps + taste, and write the hypothesis down so you can look back at it later.

On iterations vs net-new, Luke's own preference is more net-new ads, plus strong variations that are different enough to matter to the algorithm. Iterations are the safer bet.

Patterns: what keeps showing up in winners

A pattern is any significant element you keep seeing in winning (or losing) ads: an actor, a format, a style, a concept, a mechanism, a copy beat. Ask of each ad: why is this working? Why is this kind of working? Why is this not working? Then form and test a hypothesis.

  1. Put your top spenders and new ads with signs of life side by side (20 or so).
  2. Break each into its anatomy (Start here).
  3. Ask what they share: "three winners mention this concept," "two use this angle."
  4. Hypothesize why, then build the next ads on it. Example: maybe they don't care about the format at all and are responding to something new, so the next batch tries more novel formats.

"We're not in a scientific environment, so hold your patterns loosely": look for the variables strong enough to survive the noise.

Taste is the third input: a sixth sense you build by working with one brand long enough to get winners ("this will work, this won't"). You can't always explain it, which is why it's worth training deliberately.

Internal vectors (Luke's favourite source): dump your winning and losing ads into AI, ask what the patterns are, then add your own read. AI alone tends to find the same ideas again; your feedback gives it a new seed.

The production plan: an example week of 10 ads

  1. Decide the split: e.g. 3 variations on the current winner (new conceits on its concept), 2 swipes, 2 segment transplants, 3 wildcards. Choose the awareness mix too: e.g. 6 unaware or problem aware, 4 solution or product aware.
  2. Run STORMING (1a) until you have at least 30 ideas for 10 slots (Luke). Leftovers become next week's bank.
  3. Pick the 10 that best fit the plan, and record the hypothesis for each.

Why document hypotheses: you can explain your thinking to a client, and look back at it later. Over time they stack up and show which ones held, which gets close to a scientific method for advertising.

Spear vs net: sometimes you hunt a specific idea from your analysis (spear); sometimes you scan swipes and organic content for whatever is strongest (net). Do both, so you don't miss a banger by staring too hard at your own plan.

Time split for the week (Luke): about 20% analysis (reading top spenders and new ads with signs of life into a running "winners" doc), 70% ideation (this page plus STORMING, ending in one-paragraph briefs), 10% writing. Most people get this backwards.

Mix by account stage (our rule)

Account Mix
New, or few winners Mostly net-new concepts, borrowed from the market's long-runners. For a new offer in a known category, capture existing demand first with solution- and product-aware ads
Some winners with clear gaps Roughly half variations, half new
Mature, with fresh winners About 70–75% variations, 25–30% new, never zero

Where this differs from Luke: he prefers more net-new than variations, and only variations big enough to look new to the algorithm. Our mature-account split leans on variations because our own data backs proven-first (TCC hook swaps hit at about 25% within a family). Concept (Luke): the one core idea of each ad

The concept is the single idea the ad communicates, e.g. "too much vitamin D is bad for you." Write it in plain caveman words. If you can't, the ad has no point yet. Every ad has (or should have) one concept, and the hook usually expresses it. Knowing the concept is how you see which ideas the market responds to.

Pain points aren't a concept. In a mature market everyone already knows the pains and the benefits. There's no edge in writing them slightly better. An ad needs an insight: a reason to exist, a new idea about the problem or the solution.

The same concept through different lenses. "Too much vitamin D is bad for you" could be:

  • a conspiracy about the vitamin D industry
  • a whistleblower
  • two people, one with and one without, and what happened to each

Worked ED example: DirectMax "What it's not" (OS-5436 video, OS-5435 static, launched Oct 25, 2026; results pending). It opens on the pack with "It's not a supplement. Or a gas-station pill." Rules for negation ads:

  • Cross out categories, never named brands ("a pill you swallow", "generic sildenafil", "a supplement"), and never claim superiority you can't support.
  • Every "not" must be true and verifiable for the offer (formulation, route, prescription process, billing).
  • Make it a structure, not a tic: one negation list per ad. Luke's editing rules flag a repeated "This isn't X, it's Y" rhythm as AI-sounding.
  • End on what it is, concretely. A list of nots with a vague reveal wastes the setup.

Mechanism: UMP and UMS

Every mechanism has two halves: the UMP (unique mechanism of the problem: why do I have this problem?) and the UMS (unique mechanism of the solution: why is this the right fix for that?).

The best mechanisms are simple, intuitive and new. Usually one problem and one solution. The more you tie in a coherent story (the founder, how the product was made, unique ingredients or features, characterizations, a moment in time), the stronger the positioning.

Copy instincts: build the world where your product is the obvious answer. Ask: "What would have to be true about the world for this to be the best possible solution?" Then work backwards:

  1. What's most unique about this product (ingredient, combination, delivery, spokesperson, packaging)?
  2. How can those elements be preeminent: the most, the best, a category of one?
  3. Which associations and "magical thinking" make them feel even more special? (Luke's example: salmon PDRN reframed as Korean cherry salmon, whose skin survives the swim upstream.)
  4. Why is this combination right for this time and place (culture, zeitgeist)?
  5. Does it all lock together coherently?

Psychology over facts: start from what would be most psychologically compelling to the prospect, then find the facts that make it true. Don't lie or mislead. Just don't limit the search to the ingredient list.

Luke's Unique Mechanism SOP

Why a mechanism: it's the proof (a logical reason it works), the differentiation in crowded markets, the new reason to believe for jaded prospects, the curiosity, and the paradigm shift that makes your product the only logical choice. Without one you're a "me too" product competing on price or hype.

What makes a good UMP and UMS:

Part Key elements
UMP (why the problem exists) A hidden cause · specificity (names, numbers, data) · relatability (it feels intuitively right)
UMS (how the product fixes it) A unique process or ingredient · logical flow from the UMP ("if X is the problem, the fix is Y, which is what this does") · proof

Which half to lead with (the pain matrix):

Market Lead with
High pain, low awareness The UMP (they need the hidden cause first)
High pain, high awareness The UMS (they know the problem; show why this fix is different)
Low pain, high awareness Aspiration and transformation

Three places to find one: (1) the zeitgeist (swipes, market sentiment, people ahead of the curve, hard research); (2) the problem and solution people already intuit (the "interoceptive" mechanism: what they feel happening in their body); (3) what's unique about the product, pushed to superlatives (only, most, first). Luke's "little big idea" question: what's new about this product that lets them get what they want without doing what they don't want to do? (e.g. "Lunchbreak Millionaire": get rich trading in 20 minutes a day without quitting the job). Then run the "so what?" test: if it doesn't make someone curious or excited, go back.

Paradigm shift vs a little better: jaded, sophisticated markets need the whole problem reframed; less sophisticated markets just need an incremental improvement.

The Universal Mechanism Matrix (What × How):

WHAT it's about HOW it goes wrong (or gets fixed)
Structure: a physical part (mitochondria, the market's structure) Too much / too little (too much cortisol, too little liquidity)
Function: what that part does (mitochondria make energy) Out of balance (metabolism out of sync with circadian rhythm)
Element: a specific piece (a hormone like GLP-1, an indicator like RSI) Dysfunctional (mitochondria "stuck in sleep mode")

Combine one of each: mitochondria (structure) × stuck in sleep mode (dysfunctional) → "The Mitochondria Wake-Up Call".

Naming the mechanism (a characterization): the name is the hook, builds credibility, creates curiosity and sticks. Make it simple, intuitive, new, specific and aspirational. Combine a familiar word with a specific one ("Adrenal Reset Protocol"), use power words (Protocol, Matrix, Reset, Activate, Ignite, Hidden), a metaphor ("Fat Switch"), a number ("5-Minute…") or alliteration, and use the market's own words. Say it out loud: if it doesn't roll off the tongue, change it.

Common mistakes: generic ("all-natural"), over-complicated, no proof, not tied to the UMP, ignoring the market's language, never tested.

Insight vectors: how to generate insights on demand

People carry mental models of how the world works ("X causes Y," "more A means more B"). An insight finds a weak spot in that model and adds a new piece. Common vectors:

Vector Example shape
Reverse causation "Your weight slows your metabolism, not the other way round"
Multiple causes "It's not one thing, it's A, B and C together"
Hidden constraint "The real limit isn't willpower, it's X"

Intensification is the beat that follows: show how the problem compounds over time if nothing changes, and how the benefit compounds once it's fixed.

Writing rules: open on action, evidence or confrontation · give characters agency, so their choices cause the next complication · vary the tension (a discovery, a changed interpretation, a hard decision) · let props, gestures and silence carry information · keep exchanges short; subtext beats explaining every emotion · pay off questions within the ad (no serial cliffhanger).

Three routes: acted (competing goals drive the dialogue) · narrated (the narrator adds interpretation while visuals show events; don't describe everything twice) · sung (a Suno song; each verse advances the story).

Techniques worth carrying into every story

  • Event-led opening: start at the moment something happens; the second sentence develops or flips the tension.
  • Small event, real stakes: specific actions and avoided moments, not tragedy for its own sake.
  • Backstory earns its space: if removing it doesn't hurt the turn, cut it.
  • A credible messenger: the explanation matches what the speaker could actually know.
  • Picture the explanation: a short analogy for the mechanism (it doesn't replace proof).
  • Plant and pay off: an object, place or phrase from the opening returns at the end.
  • Fiction stays fiction: a dramatized scene is never presented as a real customer testimonial.

Angle rules from live ad reviews

These come from Marcus's reviews of the DirectMax story ads (Oct 21–23, 2026): eight drafts, from songs to a sitcom to micro-dramas. Check every story angle against them before writing lines or spending on production.

Premise

  1. Simple enough to say in one line, dramatic enough to stop the scroll. If the setup needs explaining ("why would the kids give them an envelope with a room number?"), it's too clever. Cut it.
  2. Believable before dramatic. Ask whether a real person would do this. "His wife announces into the microphone that he hasn't touched her" fails. "She rubs his back and says don't worry about it" passes, because it happens in every house.
  3. Pick the wound the avatar actually feels. For ED in men 55+, that's pity, the shame of hiding it, and the fear that she'll leave or thinks he doesn't want her. Infidelity by the wife is bolder but less true for long marriages, and it reads as humiliating.
  4. Moral clarity: the protagonist keeps his self-respect. Don't reward bad behavior, e.g. "now that he performs, she stops cheating." He should earn the turn through courage (telling the truth, facing it), not just a pill.

Hook 5. Open inside the moment, on a confrontation or a gut-punch line, not a mood shot, a prop or narrated backstory. The first frame and line make sense with no context. "A hand holding a pill under a clock" was rejected; a 2 a.m. kitchen confrontation and "Hey… it's okay. Don't worry about it" both work. The first frame is the most charged visual in the story: a character caught in the consequence, readable with the sound off. Example: the wife frozen at the back door at 2 a.m., heels in her hand. Marcus picked it as the best hook over the wide establishing shot. 6. Name the problem early. If the viewer can't tell within the first seconds what it's about (ED), the story isn't working ("the second two don't really explain about ED").

Stakes 7. One stakes beat, unmistakable and visual. Raise the stakes once, clearly: what could be lost, shown on screen. For example, she's crying alone in her car with a packed overnight bag on the seat: "I love you. But I don't know how much longer I can do this." Supporting scenes that only echo the problem (a cashier, a grandkid) are distractions. Cut them. 8. Public or important events raise stakes only if they're believable (a reunion slow dance, a family party). Never force a public confession.

Turn and offer 9. The reversal flips the other character's interpretation. She thinks he doesn't want her, or that he's cheating; the truth is shame about ED. It's his moment of courage. 10. The offer arrives through a motivated decision (the online doctor; ideally they decide together), not as a rescue from nowhere. Claims go to the narrator or on-screen text, never to an actor playing a doctor.

Payoff 11. Mirror the opening with a planted detail that returns changed. "Don't worry about it" flips from her pity to his confidence; the packed bag gets unpacked. 12. Cut any scene whose purpose isn't instantly obvious (a "next morning" kitchen scene with no time cue was confusing). End on the payoff, then a spoken CTA.

Fit and execution 13. Match the format to awareness, and write the same story at each level. - Problem-aware: he's hiding it and never treated it. - Solution-aware: the pill he took failed him. - Product-aware: he scrolled past the ad. 14. Match the music, setting and cast to the audience data. Region, age and gender, not just the persona label. For ED: men 55+ in the South and Midwest → country or heartland, small-town settings, a couple around 60. 15. Nothing sexually explicit, even implied (platforms reject it and AI tools block it). The emotion carries the scene. 16. Show the premise before production: a one-line premise, the beat table and the key lines. It's cheaper to fix a beat than a render.

Reusable story brief

Offer and next step · audience and the one selling idea · what's fixed vs being tested · runtime and route · protagonist's want and hidden concern · the other character's want or misreading · opening event and visible clue · complication · seeded reversal and decision · product bridge and the proof it needs · payoff, returning detail, CTA · beat-by-beat shot plan (line, action, reaction, duration).

Before production: does the opening make sense with no backstory? Does every beat change something? Was the reveal set up? Does the ending answer the main question? Is it intelligible without sound? Is every product fact accurate for this offer?

Winning Ad Styles — the list

A style is the filter the ad is rendered through. Same script, same claims, same offer, same structure, different picture: Pixar, camcorder, Notes app, courtroom sketch. Nothing the buyer has to believe changes when you swap it, which is why it's the cheapest swap there is. You are not writing a new ad. You are re-rendering a proven one.

The one-second test: could you re-render the exact same script in this and change nothing but the picture? Yes → style. No, the script has to change shape → that's a conceit (workflow 3). Entries marked (both) are honestly both.

Three rules. 1. Match the skin to the awareness: cold traffic wants native, ugly, low-fidelity (camcorder, Notes app, raw UGC); product-aware traffic can carry polish. 2. Style breaks are a tool: cutting from cartoon to real footage at the product reveal says "the story part is over, this part is real." 3. Some styles do the targeting for you: a reggae track, a country kitchen, an anime skin pick the audience before a word lands.

G. Composite and mixed reality

Two worlds in one frame.

  1. X-ray / see-through overlay — the body goes transparent on the problem area · mechanism made visible, "here's the compression."
  2. MRI / scan reading — a black-and-white scan, green highlights on the problem area, a cursor moving like a clinician's pointer · the radiology-walkthrough look; authority without a face.
  3. Pain VFX overlay — fire on the feet, lightning up the leg, ants crawling on skin, composited on real footage · makes an invisible symptom visible in one second.
  4. Thermal overlay on a real person — heat map only where it matters · inflammation, circulation, cooling.
  5. AR annotation — floating labels and arrows tracking a real object · demos, "what each part does."
  6. Thought bubbles on real footage — comic bubbles over a person's head · inner monologue, "what she's actually thinking."
  7. Real person in a cartoon world — live actor, drawn background · disarming, "let me explain simply," style break built in.
  8. Cartoon in the real world — animated character over real footage · mascot explainers, the "little guy" who tells the truth.
  9. CGI creature in live footage — a human-sized talking rat in a real driveway, a real reporter · the adversary can testify; absurd enough to stop, real enough to trust.
  10. Anthropomorphic ingredient or product — the seed-oil droplet in a waistcoat, supplement tubs arguing on a podcast, a panel of personified turkey necks · the villain or the product explains itself; carries heavy claim loads as comedy.
  11. Split reality — one frame, two versions of the same person · "you with vs without," parallel lives.
  12. Green-screen over a movie scene — creator inside a famous scene · commentary, "this scene is every guy at 40."
  13. Dubbed / redubbed — real footage, new voice, intentionally off-sync · humor, "what the doctor really means."
  14. Picture-in-picture reaction — a face reacting to their own footage · testimonials watching their "before."
  15. Style break at the reveal — cartoon, sketch, or game look for the story, hard cut to raw UGC at the product · the trust hand-off; use on any drawn or rendered style above.

How to use this tonight

  1. Take a winner, or anything with real signal. Freeze the script.
  2. Pick five styles from the list your account has never run.
  3. Brief each one: same words, same order, same offer; only the rendering changes. Note where a style break belongs, if anywhere.
  4. Render and launch. Every one is one swap from proven, so you know what the test measures.
  5. A style wins → it's the next winner in; pick five more.

4a. Hooks

Sources: Luke's docs on paradoxical questions and Jenny Hoyos' shorts (sources/luke-iha-docs/), the 4-step "deep fry" hook process, 8 vicious hook rules, 6 curiosity triggers, unaware ads masterclass, and Marcus's Luke Iha hook workshop transcript.

The hook is roughly 90% of the messaging. Spend most of your writing time on it, and learn to write hooks by hand: a few hundred reps and you can write them in seconds. Once the hook is right, AI can write most of the rest.

The four steps

Step What to do
1. Isolate the idea Write the ad (or a basic hook), then ask "what am I actually trying to say?" Put it in caveman words: "hot celebrities don't drink coffee," "menopause strips collagen." Rate it. If it's obvious (or the market already believes they know it), it needs work.
2. Commit to it One idea per hook. Don't hedge by adding more ideas. "Can you guess what these hot celebrities have in common? None of them drink coffee" is already two ideas.
3. Deep fry it Make it the most bizarre, extended, extreme version that still has a thread back to the truth, then walk it back.
4. Make it bang Strongest material first, specific, active voice, one to three emotional trigger words, an open loop.

Mindset for step 3: use associative, squishy logic (not strict logic); don't be a nerd (psychology over facts); use the tightrope effect (say something so bold they have to watch you pay it off); go crazy first, then walk it back. Most ads die from boring ideas, not wild ones. Never lie or mislead: the body has to pay the hook off honestly.

Deep-fry moves:

Move Example
Good thing → bad Zinc "helps immunity" → too much zinc starves you of copper → the "zinc flu"
Bad thing → good "I'm the worst dad of all time, and I'm proud of it"
Ineffective → harmful "It doesn't just not work, it makes it worse" (only if true)
Identity contrast "My grandma's weird naked magnesium trick"
Reverse causation "If you're diabetic, your cells are starving for sugar"
Types of types (nuance) Brown fat vs grey fat; "dermal menopause"
Rename (characterization) "Naked magnesium," "joint drought," "ice water trick"

Underneath them: contrast, surprise, nuance and stakes create attention.

Luke's magnesium example: magnesium cream is better than capsules → it absorbs through skin because it has no molecule attached → it's "bare" → "naked magnesium" → "My grandma used this weird naked magnesium trick that fixed her back pain in two weeks." The grandma/naked contrast, "weird" and "naked" as charged words, and "trick" as an open loop make it work.

The 8 vicious hook rules

A vicious hook grabs a feeling the reader already has (a fear, a shame, an unspoken dread) and squeezes it in the very first line. Most people start their ad three or four sentences in. A polite or comfortable hook is a dead hook.

  1. Relevant in the first line (first 10–20 words). Three ways in: pain or condition callout (including a mechanism they already believe in, like cortisol); belief callout (take a side in a debate they're already having: "they told you red meat would clog your arteries"); general openness (soap-opera intrigue, the hardest and biggest bets).
  2. Emotionally charged language: banned, sexless, stink, lying, die, naked, killing, terrifying, beg.
  3. Real open loops: if they can guess the answer, they won't read on.
  4. Stakes and rubbernecking: something heavy is on the line.
  5. Specific and tangible, like seasoning: enough to feel real, not so much it crowds the line.
  6. Doesn't read like an ad: news, a story, a confession, an insight.
  7. Caveman, Germanic words: sweat, not perspire; bad, not severe; sleepless, not insomnia.
  8. Consequence first, mechanism later. "This morning habit causes electrical imbalance" means nothing to them; "this morning habit spikes cortisol" does.

Six forms of tangible curiosity

Source: Luke's 6 curiosity triggers video.

Tangible curiosity is curiosity attached to something concrete: a river, a quiz, a nickname, a test you can do on your own body. Luke cycles through all six forms before writing any new ad, lead or email; hooks, fascinations and framing all get better once he has.

Why it works: marketing has four jobs: get attention, shift beliefs, remove constraints, and increase desire (away from pain or toward what they want). People won't spend effort working out a new idea, so tangible curiosity gets the attention and makes the new belief click. Sophisticated markets like weight loss do it, mostly by accident; do it on purpose, and it works even better where nobody does it.

Unaware hooks: high curiosity, low-to-medium relevance

Unaware ads lead with something highly curious that's only loosely tied to their problem: "My Uber driver said five words that changed my view on men," "In 1910, Rockefeller funded a report that reshaped American medicine." The more specific the relevance ("to your body" → "to your blood sugar" → "to your neuropathy"), the narrower the audience; the more open it is, the stronger the curiosity has to be.

Ten types that keep working: paradoxes · lost wisdom / forgotten remedy · conspiracy · warning sign · everyday threat (surprising culprit) · story · big win (the benefit behind the benefit) · big horror · symptom callout · sacred cow (validate or challenge a belief).

Built-in curiosity triggers: NASA, Harvard, the Nobel Prize, the Amish, Japan, the Swiss, monks, tribes, ancient Egyptians, Navy SEALs, the CIA, blue zones.

Paradoxical questions ("reality's greatest plot twists")

Luke calls this the single most powerful question in marketing (learned from Stefan Georgi). It's a "why" question that exposes a contradiction in how people think the world works, so the brain can't stop trying to resolve it. Classic examples: the Wall Street Journal's "two men" letter (same everything, one became president: what made the difference?), Pimsleur ("why do kids learn languages easily when adults struggle?"), and "why do some animals live so much longer than others?"

Build one: start with an unstoppable fact, either something they already accept (kids learn languages easily; morning breath is worse) or a new fact you show them (2,500-year-old teeth that look better than ours). Then create a wrinkle:

Wrinkle Shape Example
Causation Same conditions, different outcomes, because of a hidden variable Two identical men, one reads the Journal
Pattern break A rule that usually holds, then breaks Bigger animals live longer, except the tiny clam that lives 500 years
Input ≠ output More effort, less result Why do longer hours get less work done?
Context violation A different time or place flips it Why does a tribe with no dentists have perfect teeth?

Then link the hidden variable to your mechanism. The full list of 500+ examples across 100 niches is in sources/luke-iha-docs/500+ Paradoxical Questions.txt. Paradoxical questions are also the first of the 10 selling keys (4b).

Short-form hooks: Jenny Hoyos' five steps

Luke's breakdown of the creator who "solved" YouTube Shorts (useful for organic-style and top-of-funnel ads):

  1. The un-hook: relatable, almost boring hooks with universal themes (money, family) that imply something is coming ("My mom's never had a Mother's Day gift. I'm going to change that"). Top of funnel wants the broadest audience.
  2. Foreshadowing: open a loop right after the hook that promises a payoff ("…then compare them to see which one's better").
  3. Loop endings: an abrupt or twisted ending that pays off the premise, so viewers don't drop off before the end.
  4. "But / therefore" storytelling (South Park): every beat is a problem (BUT) or a response to it (THEREFORE), never "and then". It cuts flat beats and creates twists.
  5. Viral bait: interesting visuals, cliffhangers, surprise endings, irony, emotion, relatable situations, controversy, humor.

Rules that override the method

  • If you're swiping, just swipe. The market has spoken: use the exact hook.
  • Same for organic outliers: borrow the hook as-is (see 1a · Research the market).
  • Not every hook needs to be crazy. Sometimes picking the right format makes the hook fall out on its own ("here's what happens when…").
  • Our account rule: give a proven body 8–15 new hooks before writing a new body, and test viral hooks only on a proven body.

Writing the ad: hook first, then body

You need three lists: winning hooks (the first one or two lines), winning ads, and winning headlines.

  1. Hook: give the AI your winning hooks plus the raw idea → ask for 10–20 hooks like these → "more like #3 and #7" → edit by hand. This is 70–80% of the writing effort.
  2. Body: the chosen hook + the raw idea + winning ads → "write an ad that starts with this hook, modelled on these." Light edits only. Spend your editing on the hook and the opening.
  3. Headlines: winning headlines + hook + body → options → pick.
  4. Creative: statics through the static process (3d · CASTS and statics); videos go to an editor with a winning reference.

4b. Copy blocks, proof, selling keys and leads

Sources: Luke's docs on copy blocks ("legos"), the 10 selling keys and beat maps, and leads (sources/luke-iha-docs/), plus creative strategy in 15 minutes, the 22 proof weapons, copy instincts pt. 1.

Once the idea and angle are chosen (3a) and the hook is written (4a), the body copy is built from these blocks.

Copy blocks: the building blocks of persuasion

Block What it does
Pain What they want to get rid of, in lived detail
Promise What they want to gain, anchored to one core transformation
Proof The reason to believe; pair it with every promise
Curiosity What's interesting and new: usually the mechanism, the bridge from pain to promise
Constraints What holds them back (time, money, effort, beliefs), removed
Conditions Urgency, scarcity, discounts: why now

Use them like a checklist: more blocks, and stronger ones, usually make a stronger ad. Most copy feedback comes down to adding a missing block or strengthening a weak one.

The canyon metaphor: the prospect stands on Pain Plateau and wants the Promised Land across the canyon. Curiosity (the mechanism) is the bridge, proof is the trust that lets them take the first step, constraints are the baggage they must drop, and conditions are why they have to cross now.

Five kinds of constraint (Luke's examples for "make $10k/mo as a copywriter"): identity ("I'm not a good writer") · experience ("cold outreach doesn't work") · belief ("you have to be extroverted") · value ("I don't want to sell scammy products") · resource ("no time outside my job"). Time and money are the two biggest; people with money lack time, and people with time lack money.

Three ways to use the blocks:

  1. Fix a headline by adding blocks. "How to go from struggling freelancer to $10k+/mo" (pain + promise) → add a constraint ("…without cold outreach or job boards") → add curiosity (a named method) → add credibility ("9-figure copywriter reveals…").
  2. Fix weak copy by sharpening blocks, not adding them: make each one more precise and more relevant to the market ("date more women" → a specific outcome; "scientists" → a credible, specific messenger; "3-step method" → a named system). Accuracy and precision beat hype.
  3. Grade it with copy velocity: number of blocks ÷ number of words × 100. Five blocks in 61 words scores 8; the same five blocks in 19 words scores 26. Say the same thing in fewer words. It's a guide, not a law: don't compress it into nonsense.

Proof: 22 types in 5 categories

Four rules: (1) match the proof to the size of the promise; (2) the harder proof is to fake, the stronger it is; (3) let them experience it themselves when possible; (4) psychological proof, making it feel true, is the most underrated kind.

Category Types
Psychological Technical jargon (a quick flurry, used sparingly) · candid communication (admit a flaw) · explanatory reasoning · logical argument (math) · guarantees
Experiential Testable proof ("check this right now") · demonstration · trials and samples · challenges · comparisons · before and after
Empirical Studies (and say why the source matters) · stats · infographics
Credible Third-party certifications · track record · expert endorsements · press · awards
Social Testimonials and reviews · case studies · social media metrics

Place proof where doubt creeps in: right after a big claim, right before a call to action. Proof isn't about convincing; it's about making them feel safe buying.

Regulated categories (health, ED, finance) restrict claims, testimonials and certifications. Check the account's compliance notes before using any proof type.

2 Surprising culprit The problem behind the problem "It isn't UV or genetics, it's glycation…"

Leads: the first 600–800 words

Source: Luke's leads video and his leads SOP doc.

The lead is the opening of a VSL or sales letter: usually 600–800 words (up to about 1,200 in a 30–60 minute VSL), before the background story, credibility, UMP, UMS, product creation, close and FAQ. It's the part most people see, so it has the biggest effect on conversion: Luke has seen 40%+ lifts from changing a few words.

Two ways to think about it:

  • A movie trailer. Show the best bits without giving away the plot. Explain too much and they feel they already know it, and leave.
  • A sales letter for the sales letter. The ad sells the lead, the lead sells the VSL, the VSL sells the product. Each piece only has to win the next few seconds.

Structure: a micro-lead (the hook, 50–200 words) → the lead body (hit most of the elements below, in almost any order) → a CTA to keep reading or watching. Get the micro-lead right, hit the checklist and don't run long, and the rest is close to a template.

Lead elements, by copy block:

Element Copy block
Call out the problem Pain
Promise the solution Promise
Tease the story (one sentence is enough) · tease the contrarian angle · tease the mechanism · a unique nickname · fascinations Curiosity
Address skepticism · credibility · testimonials Proof
"Even if / without" Constraints
Qualifiers (who it's for) · why watch now Conditions
Introduce the speaker · future pacing · failed solutions / common advice is wrong · the transition Connectors

The best leads weave these together rather than going block by block. You don't need all of them.

Rough proportions: micro-lead 5–10% · hook phase (the main promise plus the mechanism) 10–15% · build belief (proof, credibility, testimonials, empathy) 15–20% · handle objections 20–25% · close (urgency to keep watching, plus curiosity) 20–25%. Guidelines, not absolutes.

It's fractal: for a shorter piece, divide everything by about four. An 8,000-word VSL has a 600–800-word lead; a 2,000-word one has a 150–200-word lead.

The finer points:

  • Open with high curiosity. The micro-lead is the most important part of the lead, and most are boring ("If you're X, this is the most important letter you'll ever read"). Use the eight openings in 4a · Hooks.
  • End on fascinations (optional): two to four curiosity bullets ("I'll reveal the #1 hidden cartilage destroyer…"). They're extra shots on goal for anyone the opening didn't fully hook.
  • Tell them to keep watching. "People are compliant": say "make sure you watch to the end."
  • Speak to the core wound, not just the benefit. For joint pain the deepest fear is losing options, so the line isn't "you'll feel great," it's "every possibility opens back up." Use their felt sense: constricted, then free.
  • Take a jab at their enemy (anti-aging: "energy-drink-slamming millennials scratching their heads").
  • You can break the formula. Luke's direct warm-traffic workshop letter skips the structure, but underneath it still has the pain, a named mechanism, the outcome and a reason to act now.

Common mistakes: getting to the promise too slowly; running too long (his goldfish lead is 478 words; he'd cut his own 235-word micro-lead in half); giving away the plot; a weak opening.

Writing one with AI (Luke's workflow): give a lead bot the speaker's background and credibility, the story of the problem, the characterizations, testimonials, failed solutions and fascinations → it writes the lead → ask for 10 micro-lead ideas, pick one, constrain it to 100–200 words with no visual description → trim by hand. The AI always runs long (his came out at 1,500 words and needed cutting to about 1,000).

The standard

Luke's bar is 50–100 good ads a week: about 10 ads in a 2–3 hour sprint, with a timer. Constraints stop over-editing. Only around 10–20% of ads will win, so failure is the rule: you get better through reps, not by perfecting one ad. The week's time split (20% analysis, 70% ideation, 10% writing) is in step 2; the writing itself is in step 4.

Deliverable rules

Every ad meets the account's standing deliverable rules (for ED: burned-in subtitles, safe-zone text, one product per ad, verified ingredients). Labels, batch sizing and launch rules are in 6 · Launch and test.

How production works (high level)

There are three routes from an approved brief to a finished file. All of them end the same way: one video per ad with burned-in captions, text inside the Meta Reels safe zone, the same file used on TikTok and Meta.

Route When The steps
Static Image ads Pick the idea (template types or SCRAWLS, 3d) → find a reference image → reverse-engineer it into a prompt → generate (Nano Banana / GPT image / Midjourney) → add headline and product → check text and claims
Editor Live action, UGC, heavy edits Write the brief (creative-brief-generator or bad-creative-brief) → Brief Intake → the editor gets the brief plus a winning reference → review against the brief and the deliverable rules
In-house AI video Animation, restyles, AI UGC, songs Below

The in-house AI video pipeline:

  1. Script and shot plan: hook, body and the shot list with timing (the brief).
  2. Stills: generate the characters and key frames (Higgsfield Soul, Gemini/Nano Banana) and keep the characters consistent across shots.
  3. Motion: animate the stills into clips (Veo 3 via Google Flow, Kling, Seedance). Kling's edit mode can restyle an existing winner (e.g. the 4437 body as claymation) without re-shooting.
  4. Voice and music: voiceover (ElevenLabs, Gemini narration) or a song (Suno, Lyria); lock the audio before timing the shots.
  5. Edit and assemble: stitch the clips to the audio, retime each shot, add the opening card, product shots and B-roll. Done with an assemble.py script per ad (ffmpeg) or in CapCut.
  6. Captions: transcribe with the approved script, then render burned-in captions in the brand style (ai-media captions: preview once, reuse the style).
  7. QA: check frames for text in the safe zone, caption timing against the audio, product and claim accuracy, and no AI artifacts (Stefan's editing checks in 7). Then upload.

Where the tools live: the ai-media command-line tool (images, narration, music, Kling video, captions, offline price quotes) and the per-ad production/<ad>/assemble.py scripts are in the ED project. The skills are listed below.

6. Launch and test: launching a batch you can learn from

Sources: Marcus's account rules (V2 Winner System and account notes), Luke's creative strategy in 15 minutes and what "strategy" means (diversity, hypotheses), 7 hidden sources for statics (creative diversity under Andromeda), Stefan Georgi's post-mortem (portfolio mix).

Testing turns the batch from 5 · Ad production into evidence. The goal isn't only to find a winner; it's to know why something won, so the next batch is smarter.

Before launch: set the rules for this account

Write these down once per account (the ED project keeps them in ED-CREATIVE-NOTES.md):

Rule What to define
Bottom-line metric What makes a real winner: CAC, ROAS, cost per sale
Leading metrics, in order The cheap early signals you judge first (e.g. cost per click or quiz start → cost per lead → cost per sale)
Spend floor How much an ad must spend before you call it
Pause rules When a test stops (e.g. no lead by a set spend; CPA above a ceiling after the first sales)
Winner threshold The number that makes an ad a winner (and a "leading winner" that only wins on early metrics)

Label every ad at launch

Label Example
Track Variation · repair · net-new
One swap from The winner, swipe, comment or organic hook it borrows from
Knob turned The one element that changed (hook, conceit, actor, style, segment…)
Persona and awareness e.g. "M1 aging husband · solution aware"
Hypothesis Why we think it will work, in one sentence
Decides it The bottom-line metric and threshold

Tag the anatomy too (segment, concept, angle, format, CASTS), ideally in the ad name, so patterns are visible later (2 · Decide the batch).

Size and structure the batch

  • Fund tests properly. Fewer ads that each reach the spend floor beat many starved ones. If the budget can't carry every ad to the floor, make the batch smaller.
  • Turn one knob per variation, and name it, so the result is readable. A combined change is logged as combined.
  • Compare like with like. Test a variation next to its parent, in the same or an identical ad group. Group context alone can shift results 1.5–2× (seen in the ED account).
  • Make net-new ads genuinely different. Meta's Andromeda system rewards creative diversity. Same actor, same room, new words can read as the same ad. When the goal is new reach, change visible signals together (actor, setting, first frame) and cover different segment × awareness buckets.
  • Keep a balanced portfolio. Mostly proven-based work with a deliberate share of swings: by account stage (see 2 · Decide the batch), or Stefan's 80/20.
  • Expect most to fail. Roughly 10–20% of ads win. A failed test with a clear label is still useful.

While it runs

  • Judge leading metrics first, then sales; don't kill an ad before its spend floor unless a pause rule says so.
  • Don't edit a live test mid-flight. Launch a new variant instead, so the comparison stays clean.
  • Watch for delivery problems (rejections, no spend) separately from performance. An ad that never delivered hasn't been tested.

Hand-off to evaluation

When a test reaches its floor or a pause rule, record the result against its label (metric, spend, verdict) and move to 7 · Evaluate and iterate.

What happened Path

Knowing whether an ad won is easy. The real work is knowing why it won or lost, so you can keep, iterate or drop the hypothesis behind it.

The metrics to look at

Read them top to bottom: attention, then clicks, then conversion, then the bottom line. A good number high up doesn't make a winner; the bottom line does.

Stage Metric What it tells you
Attention Hook rate (3-second views ÷ impressions) Did the first frame and first line stop the scroll?
Hold rate (ThruPlays or 15-second views ÷ 3-second views) Did the body keep them watching?
CPM What the audience costs to reach; rising CPMs can sink a good ad
Click Link CTR, cost per click Did the ad make them want to act?
Conversion Cost per landing-page view, cost per quiz start or lead, conversion rate Did the page and funnel match the ad's promise?
Bottom line CAC / CPA, ROAS, then refunds, retention and LTV where tracked Did it make money? This decides the winner

Reading a weak spot:

Pattern Likely problem Try
Low hook rate The opening New hooks or a new first frame on the same body (4a)
Good hook rate, low hold The body after the hook Tighten the body; check the hook-to-body join; Stefan's editing checks
Good hold, low CTR The offer or call to action Strengthen proof, constraints, the offer and the CTA (4b)
Good CTR, low conversion The landing page or funnel Make the page match the ad's promise; check tracking
Converts, but CAC too high Cost to reach, or the audience CPMs, segment fit, a broader or different persona (1b)

Stefan's 5-step diagnosis (for ads worth investigating)

At volume (50+ new ads a month), don't post-mortem every loser. Spend the effort where you had strong conviction, where the angle has worked before or is scaling across the category, or where an execution mistake may have hidden a good angle.

Step Check If it fails
1. Format × audience fit Was the format mismatched with this demographic or offer? (e.g. song ads work in women's health and beauty, but can flop for men who want to get jacked.) If the format is proven for this brand and audience, skip ahead. Test the same concept in a proven format before throwing the angle out
2. Execution (the micro-flaws) The "FU test": does the portrayal of the prospect feel insulting ("that's not me")? Use a relatable regular guy, not a grotesque slob. Overlay overload: winning yappers had 4–5 subtle overlays in the first minute; a loser had 20. Amateur editing: giant or mistimed captions, dizzying zooms, random emojis or sparkles, cheap AI animations. Re-edit or fix the pacing. Auditing the edit before launch can lift win rates 10–15%
3. Angle track record Proven angle that failed now? Likely a weak hook or a fatigued delivery. New angle that failed once or twice? Inconclusive. Angle that has failed repeatedly for months across formats? Probably invalid. Proven: test 4–10 hook variations or another proven format. New: don't kill it yet; change the format, opening situation or lead. Exhausted: kill it, or cap it at 1 in 30 ads
4. Market validation Are competitors scaling this same angle, mechanism or pain point? If yes, the angle is valid; your entry point (hook, lead, spokesperson, offer bridge) is what failed. Iterate the entry, not the idea
5. Portfolio mix Is the batch balanced? Stefan's model: 80% proven, low-variance work; 20% high-variance swings Rebalance. Give weird formats 2–3 tries before moving on if they grab attention. (Our working mix by account stage is in 2 · Decide the batch.)

Before killing an idea, also try it in the other medium (video ↔ static).

Winner iteration: what to test next on a winner

Step 1: preserve the winner. Confirm the business result. Record its full anatomy (0 · Start here). If only the whole combination is proven, keep the whole combination as the control.

Step 2: choose the branch. (The account-level moves, such as a new style, format or segment on a winner, are ranked in step 2; this is the per-ad version.)

Branch A: how it's shown (keep the message) Branch B: what it says (keep the visuals)
A viral hook or pattern interrupt in front of the winning body (our most common first move) New opening words or copy hook
A new first frame or visual opening A new on-screen headline or text
On-screen text styling or timing Clearer wording of the same message
Delivery or opening audio A new angle or promise
A recut that moves the strongest moment to the front New proof or objection handling
UGC: a new location first, then a new actor A rewritten body section
A new format, conceit or style (CASTS) CTA wording
Static → carousel or motion

If a change touches both branches (a viral opening that changes words and visuals), log it as a combined test.

Step 3: Marcus's priority order

  1. Cheap openings first: a viral hook on the winning body (give a proven body 8–15 hooks before writing a new one); static: same message with a new visual, or same visual with new words.
  2. UGC variations: location first, then actor, same script.
  3. Broader format and messaging development: a new conceit or style, a new angle, a new body section.
  4. Persona and awareness expansion (horizontal): the same message for a new segment or facet, or a new awareness entry point.
  5. Offer or funnel expansion, as a separate experiment.

The rule underneath it all: the biggest expected benefit for the least work.

Step 4: evaluate each variation against its parent under comparable conditions. Better → it becomes the new parent. Worse → keep the control. Inconclusive → name the evidence gap. Better attention alone doesn't make a winner.

How winning concepts grow into account pillars

New idea (often a long-form static) → it gets traction → optimize the copy and creative (statics into videos, new spokespeople, hooks, formats) → build congruent funnels (advertorial, quiz) around the copy and creative pair → a scaling pillar. A few pillars carry the account, and every pillar starts as a fresh idea from a diverse source.

Account-wide decline

Check the account and funnel first (tracking, landing page, offer, budget). Then: has the market heard the claim too often (move up a sophistication stage, 1b · Persona bank)? Are the hooks recycled versions of what everyone runs? Is the hook too narrow to reach enough people? Does the first frame look like every other ad in the category?

Keep a log

For every test, record the parent and variant, the hypothesis, the one knob, what stayed fixed, the result and the decision. Over time the account's own hit rate by move (hook swap, style swap, segment transplant, net-new) should replace the default mix. Add every tested hook to the account's hook library.
